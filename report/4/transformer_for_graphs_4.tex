\title{Empirical Analysis of Transformers on Graph Connectivity \\
       \large Part IV: What Makes a Graph Hard, and a Similarity Read-out}
\author{Edoardo Paccagnella}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent We ask what makes a graph hard for a standard connectivity transformer, and
whether a non-standard read-out helps. Difficulty has two separable axes ---
distance-vs-capacity ($3^L=9$) and density-vs-training, not the spectral gap --- and a
similarity read-out $\hat R_{ij}\propto\cos(h_i,h_j)$ doubles the reach radius to
$2\cdot 3^L$ and removes the model's over-connection error, but not a bottleneck.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

We rely on two findings from the earlier reports: the \emph{data lever} does not help
a standard transformer generalise (Report~III: the outcome is seed-dominated), and an
$L$-layer model hits a sharp wall at shortest-path distance $\approx 3^L$ ($=9$ for our
$L=2$ models) --- a property of the model, not the data. We ask three things, in order:
(1) is the diameter the right notion of difficulty (Section~\ref{sec:diff}); (2) can the
spectral gap be isolated as a separate cause (Section~\ref{sec:gap}); (3) does a
\emph{spectral} read-out move the wall (Section~\ref{sec:readout}). Family-level numbers
are averaged over several seeds (four at $n=40$, eight at $n=20$); only the $n=64$ depth
sweep is single-seed.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Model.} The RoBERTa-faithful standard transformer of Report~III ($L=2$, single
head, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=512$, normalised-ReLU attention), trained online at $n=20$ for
$10^6$ steps. Two training sets: \textbf{ER} ($\mathrm{ER}(20,0.08)$, as in the paper) and
\textbf{mixed} (a uniform mixture of the families below); two read-outs (\emph{linear},
\emph{similarity}); an optional Laplacian-smoothness loss.

\paragraph{Two quantities.} The \textbf{diameter} is the largest shortest-path distance
(how far the farthest pair is). The \textbf{spectral gap} is the smallest non-zero
eigenvalue of the normalised Laplacian: small means a bottleneck (a thin bridge or long
corridor), large means a well-mixed graph.

\subsection{The graph families}
\label{sec:families}

A panel chosen to spread across diameter and gap (Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}). All graphs are
generated in \texttt{data.py} on $n$ nodes without self-loops (the self-loops and a random
node permutation are added at evaluation time). How each is built:

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.15em}
  \item \textbf{ER}: each off-diagonal edge present independently with probability $p$
        (here $p=0.08$) --- the upper triangle is a Bernoulli$(p)$ mask, then symmetrised.
        This is training set~A.
  \item \textbf{1chain}: the single path $0\!-\!1\!-\!\cdots\!-\!(n{-}1)$ --- one component,
        diameter $n{-}1$, tiny gap.
  \item \textbf{1cycle}: 1chain plus the closing edge $(0,n{-}1)$ --- the cycle $C_n$, every
        node degree $2$, diameter $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$.
  \item \textbf{2chains}: $n=2k$ nodes split into two halves $\{0,\dots,k{-}1\}$ and
        $\{k,\dots,2k{-}1\}$, each made into a path --- two components.
  \item \textbf{2cliques}: the same two halves, each made into a complete graph.
  \item \textbf{2cycle}: the same two halves, each made into a cycle $C_k$.
  \item \textbf{path\_union}: the $n$ nodes are cut at $k{-}1$ random points into
        $k\in\{1,\dots,4\}$ contiguous segments, each turned into a path. With $k=1$ this is
        a single path of all $n$ nodes (distances up to $n{-}1$); $k>1$ adds genuine
        cross-component disconnections, so ``predict all connected'' is wrong.
  \item \textbf{er\_blocks / clique\_blocks}: the same random partition into
        $k\in\{1,\dots,4\}$ blocks; each block is made a complete graph (clique\_blocks) or a
        \emph{connected} ER graph --- a random spanning path over the block (to guarantee
        connectivity) plus extra edges at probability $0.3$ (er\_blocks).
  \item \textbf{barbell}: two cliques of size $c\approx n/3$ placed at the two ends, joined
        by a single path running through all the middle nodes --- one component, but every
        path between the two cliques must cross that one bridge (the smallest gap of all).
        \emph{barbell\_var} sweeps the gap by varying $c$ at fixed shape (small $c
        \Rightarrow$ long bridge $\Rightarrow$ tiny gap; large $c \Rightarrow$ short bridge
        $\Rightarrow$ larger gap).
  \item \textbf{expander}: an approximate random $d$-regular graph ($d=3$) --- start from a
        random Hamiltonian cycle (a random node order joined consecutively, which guarantees
        connectivity and degree $2$), then add extra random near-perfect matchings up to
        degree $d$. Tiny diameter, large gap --- the structural opposite of a path.
        \emph{expander\_var} varies $d$.
  \item \textbf{chain\_plus}: a long path of $n{-}s$ nodes plus a separate short path of $s$
        nodes, with $s\sim U[3,\,n/3]$ --- two components, with within-component distances up
        to $n{-}s{-}1$ (well past $9$), so it exposes the $3^L$ wall even at $n=20$.
\end{itemize}
The mixed training set is an \emph{online} stream (graphs generated on the fly, no fixed set):
a family is drawn uniformly per sample from the nine families \emph{er, er\_blocks,
clique\_blocks, path\_union, 2chains, 2cliques, 1cycle, 2cycle, 1chain}. \emph{expander},
\emph{barbell} and \emph{chain\_plus} are held out, so they probe unseen structure. (With batch
$1000$ and $10^6$ steps the model sees $\sim10^9$ graphs in total; note that the deterministic
families --- 1chain, 1cycle, 2chains, 2cliques, 2cycle --- are a single graph each up to a node
permutation, so the model sees the same structure re-permuted, while ER, the blocks and
path\_union are genuinely varied.)

\section{Thread 1: what makes a graph hard --- the diameter and the density}
\label{sec:diff}

We want to know what makes a graph hard for the standard model (the plain linear read-out).
The metric throughout is \textbf{exact-match}: for a graph on $n$ nodes the model outputs an
$n\times n$ matrix of yes/no answers (``is node $i$ connected to node $j$?''), and exact-match
$=1$ only if that whole matrix is right. We look at three candidate explanations in turn ---
the diameter, the density, the spectral gap --- using the standard model trained on
$\mathrm{ER}(20,0.08)$ (and the matching $n=40$ model), evaluated on the families of
Section~\ref{sec:families}.

\subsection{How accuracy depends on distance: the capacity wall}
\label{sec:diffmap}

The cleanest way to see the diameter's effect is to feed the model a long chain --- a graph
that contains node pairs at \emph{every} distance --- and look at how often it gets a pair
right as a function of how far apart the two nodes are. Figure~\ref{fig:accdist} does this.
Accuracy is essentially perfect up to distance $\approx9$ and falls beyond it. The reason is
the result from Report~III: a $2$-layer model can confirm a connection only within
$3^L=3^2=9$ hops; past that it sees no short path and can no longer tell a
\emph{connected-but-far} pair from a \emph{disconnected} one, so it starts to make mistakes.
(The partial recovery on the very farthest pairs is a thing we do not fully explain and do
not rely on.) This is the main way the diameter matters: a graph whose farthest pairs sit
beyond $9$ will have wrong entries, so its exact-match drops. Figure~\ref{fig:accdist} is an
average over seeds; the per-seed numbers behind it (which seeds wall where, and which recover)
are given later in Section~\ref{sec:perseed}.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Density also matters, and it is a separate effect}
\label{sec:density}

Distance is not the only thing. The model was trained only on \emph{sparse} graphs (an
$\mathrm{ER}(20,0.08)$ graph has on average $\approx1.6$ neighbours per node). If we hand it a
much \emph{denser} graph it fails even when the diameter is tiny, simply because such a graph
is unlike anything it saw in training (out-of-distribution). We measure density by the
\textbf{mean degree} (the average number of neighbours per node). Figure~\ref{fig:accdens}
holds the diameter small (so the capacity wall is out of the picture) and varies the density:
accuracy is high near the training density and falls as the graph gets denser, very sharply
at $n=40$ (which was trained even sparser). So difficulty has (at least) two separate
ingredients: \emph{distance vs.\ the capacity} and \emph{density vs.\ the training set}. We
return to the sharpest single instance of this density failure --- the \emph{degree heuristic} on
dense 2cliques --- in Section~\ref{sec:third}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \figorbox{runs/report4/report4_figs/acc_vs_density.png}{0.72}
    \caption{Whole-graph exact-match vs mean degree (density). \emph{Same model as
    Figure~\ref{fig:accdist}} (standard, linear read-out, ER-trained; $n=20$ over $8$ seeds,
    $n=40$ over $4$ seeds, mean $\pm1$ std). \emph{Test set:} a purpose-built pool of
    single-component graphs (the difficulty-map pool: an ER density sweep, a random-regular degree
    sweep, barbells, chains and cycles), here restricted to small diameter ($\le6$) so the density
    effect is isolated from the capacity wall. Both models do well near their training density
    (dotted lines) and fail on denser graphs; the sparser-trained $n=40$ model collapses almost
    immediately.}
    \label{fig:accdens}
\end{figure}

\subsection{The spectral gap: we cannot separate it from the diameter}

The third candidate is the spectral gap (a small gap $=$ a bottleneck). The problem is that,
for ordinary sparse graphs, the gap is essentially just \emph{another name for the diameter}:
a long, thin graph has a large diameter \emph{and} a small gap, while a compact graph has a
small diameter \emph{and} a large gap. Figure~\ref{fig:collinear} plots one dot per graph
(diameter on the $x$-axis, gap on the $y$-axis): the dots fall on a single diagonal line ---
they are collinear, correlation $-0.82$ --- with no graphs in the ``large diameter and large
gap'' region. Because the two move together, no experiment on these graphs can say whether
the difficulty is caused by the diameter or by the gap.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \figorbox{runs/report4/report4_figs/diam_gap_collinear.png}{0.92}
    \caption{Where the test graphs sit in the (diameter, gap) plane ($n=20$; $n=40$ looks the
    same, correlation $-0.82$). Only \emph{connected} graphs are shown, because the diameter
    and the spectral gap are only defined for a single component --- the disconnected families
    are made of these same pieces (a 2-chain is two paths, a 2-clique two cliques, a 2-cycle
    two cycles). ER, random-regular/expander and barbell are each swept over a parameter (edge
    probability, degree, clique size), so they form clouds; 1chain, 1cycle and a single clique
    are one fixed graph each at $n=20$, hence one marker apiece. Everything except the barbell
    lies on one descending diagonal ridge (clique $\to$ ER/expander $\to$ 1cycle $\to$ 1chain):
    a larger diameter comes with a smaller gap, with nothing in the ``large diameter \emph{and}
    large gap'' corner, so the two cannot be told apart. Only the barbell (red) sits below the
    ridge --- a moderate diameter at a tiny gap --- and it gets there only by being locally
    dense (its two cliques).}
    \label{fig:collinear}
\end{figure}

The collinearity has a single exception: the \textbf{barbell} (two dense cliques joined by one
thin bridge), the red dots sitting \emph{below} the ridge in Figure~\ref{fig:collinear} --- a
moderate diameter at a tiny gap. It is the one graph that lets us ask whether a bottleneck
(a small gap) makes a graph hard \emph{beyond} what its diameter says. That question, and the
rest of the spectral-gap analysis, is the subject of Thread~2 (Section~\ref{sec:gap}); we keep
it out of this thread, which is about the two axes we \emph{can} separate.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:third-fam}

Table~\ref{tab:fam} collects the whole picture on the families. We trained \emph{eight}
copies of the baseline model (eight random seeds, ER training, linear read-out) and evaluated
each on every family; the table reports, per family, its \emph{typical} (median) diameter and
gap --- a label for where that family sits --- the mean exact-match over the eight models, and
the standard deviation of that exact-match across the eight seeds (``seed sd''). How to read
it: sorting by gap, the families with a small gap / large diameter (top of the table) are
hard and the well-mixed ones (ER, expander) are easy, which is the ``gap matters'' impression
--- but, as above, that is mostly the diameter and the density in disguise. The large
\emph{seed sd} on every structured family ($\ge0.35$) is its own warning: per seed the
exact-match is essentially all-or-nothing (a model either finds the generalising solution or
does not, the seed lottery of Report~III), so the mean is a difficulty \emph{ranking} over the
lucky seeds, not a number any single run will hit. Only ER and expander are seed-stable
($\mathrm{sd}\le0.03$).

\subsection{Results by node-pair distance}
\label{sec:perseed}

These tables measure a single \emph{pair} of nodes:
each entry is the accuracy on the node pairs whose shortest-path distance is exactly $d$ (only
connected pairs, i.e.\ ``reach''), \emph{not} a whole-graph score. 
Tables~\ref{tab:dist_chain_plus}--\ref{tab:dist_path_union} give the per-seed accuracy for the three families with
long within-component distances, for all eight ER-trained seeds and the mixed model.

\paragraph{ER-trained} For the seeds
that found the generalising solution, accuracy is $\approx1$ up to the capacity $9$, dips just
past it, and then \emph{climbs back} on the farthest pairs. On chain\_plus,
seed $1000$ falls to $0.72$ at $d=11$ and returns to $1.00$ by $d=15$; seed $3000$ never dips
at all ($1.00$ throughout). The seeds that did \emph{not} generalise ($2000,4000,5000$)
instead slide down monotonically and never recover (seed $2000$ reaches $0.07$ at $d=16$).
This is the seed lottery of Table~\ref{tab:fam} seen distance-by-distance. The recovery (the climbing back) is
also family-dependent: it is strong on chain\_plus (back to $1.00$), weaker on 1chain (seed
$3000$ bottoms at $0.69$ around $d=13$ and only reaches $0.98$ by $d=16$).

\paragraph{Mixed-trained: the wall is gone here.} The mixed model solves all three families:
$\approx1.00$ at every distance and every seed (last row of each table). Two of them (1chain,
path\_union) are in its training stream; chain\_plus is held out, but it is built from the same
chain pieces, so the model handles it too. The wall being absent at $n=20$ is \emph{not} because
the model is now unbounded --- it is because at $n=20$ the chains are too short to contain
distances far enough past $9$ to expose it. The $n=40$ tables below, where the chains are long
enough, show the wall return even on families the model was trained on.

\paragraph{The same families at $n=40$, where the capacity is clearly visible.}
Tables~\ref{tab:n40_chain_plus}--\ref{tab:n40_path_union} repeat this at $n=40$, where the
chains reach distance $36$ and the columns are placed finely around $9$ so the wall is easy to
read. Two things change. First, it is now the \emph{mixed} model that shows the wall in its
sharpest form: exactly $1.00$ up to $d=9$ and then a step down to $\approx0.55$ at $d=10$ followed by the recovery on the farthest pairs.

distances past the capacity; at $n=40$ it can, and the wall appears.) Second, the ER-trained
model is uniformly mediocre instead ($\approx0.85$--$0.97$ at all distances, no step at $9$):
never having seen the structure, it is partly right everywhere rather than perfect-then-broken.

\subsection{Per-seed detail by graph diameter}
\label{sec:bydiam}

The tables above scored individual node pairs. The complementary question is at the
\emph{whole-graph} level: as a graph's \emph{diameter} grows, how often is its \emph{entire}
connectivity matrix predicted correctly (exact-match)? A graph is exact-match $=1$ only if every
one of its pairs is right, so a graph whose diameter exceeds the capacity necessarily contains
at least one pair beyond reach and fails. We show this only for the families where the diameter
\emph{varies} (the fixed-diameter families --- 2chains, 1cycle, the cliques, barbell, \ldots ---
are a single point and uninformative here), and only the interesting ones: \textbf{path\_union}
(a sparse family whose diameter is the length of its longest path, so it is a clean diameter
sweep at fixed density) and \textbf{ER} (the training distribution).

\paragraph{path\_union.} Tables~\ref{tab:byd_pu_n20}--\ref{tab:byd_pu_n40} show the graph-level
wall directly. For the mixed model at $n=20$, exact-match is $\approx1$ for diameter up to $9$,
drops over diameters $10$--$13$ (the graph now contains pairs just past the capacity), and
\emph{recovers} to $1.00$ by diameter $16$ (the same boundary recovery, now at the graph level).
The ER-trained model shows the same shape on the seeds that generalise (e.g.\ seed $1000$:
$1.00$ at diameter $8$, $0.28$ at $10$, back to $0.99$ at $16$) and is flat at $0$ on the seeds
that do not --- the seed lottery again. At $n=40$ the wall is wider (diameter $10$--$33$ all near
$0$) with the recovery only on the very largest diameters.

\paragraph{ER (training distribution).} Table~\ref{tab:byd_er_n20} is the in-distribution
counterpart. The mixed model solves ER at every diameter it produces ($\le13$ at $n=20$, all
$\approx1$). The ER-trained model again splits by seed: the generalising seeds are $1.00$
throughout, the others decay as the diameter grows (seed $2000$: $0.89$ at diameter $9$, $0.00$
at $13$). At $n=40$ (Table~\ref{tab:byd_er_n40}) the in-distribution ER shows \emph{both} Thread~1
axes at once: even the mixed model (ER is in its training stream) \emph{declines} once the ER
diameter passes the capacity ($\approx0.92$ at $D10$, $\approx0.8$ at $D11$), the same wall as the
structured families, while the ER-trained model instead dips at the \emph{densest}, small-diameter
graphs ($\approx0.75$ at $D5$, the rare high-edge tail) and is otherwise near-perfect.

\section{Thread 2: the spectral gap --- does a bottleneck add difficulty?}
\label{sec:gap}

Thread~1 found two difficulty axes we can separate (distance-vs-capacity and
density-vs-training) and showed that on ordinary graphs the spectral gap is just collinear with
the diameter (Figure~\ref{fig:collinear}), so it says nothing new there. The one exception was
the barbell --- a real bottleneck (tiny gap) at a moderate diameter. This thread asks the gap
question directly: when a graph really does have a bottleneck, does that make it hard
\emph{beyond} what its diameter predicts, and can we measure the gap's effect on its own? We try
three constructions. Short answer: a bottleneck does add difficulty (robustly, as a direction),
but no construction isolates the gap cleanly.

\subsection{Results for barbell}

The barbell is two dense cliques joined by one thin bridge: one connected component, a moderate
diameter, and the smallest gap of all our families. It is held out of training, so it probes a
bottleneck the model never saw. Tables~\ref{tab:bar_n40}--\ref{tab:bar_n20} give its accuracy on
connected pairs by distance, per seed.

The clean result is at $n=40$ (Table~\ref{tab:bar_n40}): the mixed model gets the within-clique
pairs right ($d=1$: $0.99$) but \emph{collapses at $d=3$} ($\approx0.4$) --- four times
\emph{inside} the capacity $9$, where every non-bottleneck family is still perfect at $d\le9$.
The pairs that fail are exactly those whose only route crosses the bridge, and all four seeds
agree. (The ER-only model never saw cliques, so it is poor on the barbell at every distance,
including $d=1$.) A simple path also has a small spectral gap, but the model handles it up to distance 9. So the barbell fails not just because its spectral gap is small, but because it contains a narrow cut: one single bridge separating two dense regions. At $n=20$ (Table~\ref{tab:bar_n20}) the cliques are larger relative to $n$, so
the bridge sits further out and the collapse is at $d\approx7$--$9$ rather than $3$; the effect
is the same, only at a different distance, and the ER model shows the usual seed lottery.

\subsection{Results for barbell\_var: a sweep in the clique size}

To grade the effect, \emph{barbell\_var} varies the clique size (and so the bridge length) while
keeping the barbell shape. We sweep the full range (cliques from $2$ up to $n/2$), but on closer
inspection this does \emph{not} isolate the gap, for two reasons. First, a barbell's gap is
intrinsically tiny and barely moves: across the whole sweep it stays in $\approx0.006$--$0.019$
at $n=20$ and $\approx0.0008$--$0.005$ at $n=40$, and it is \emph{non-monotone} (U-shaped) in the
clique size --- larger at both ends (a long thin bridge, or a short bridge between big cliques)
and smallest in between. So there is no wide, monotone gap range to read off. Second, changing
the clique size changes the diameter ($3\to19$ at $n=20$) and the clique \emph{density} at the
same time.

Looking at the accuracy by the actual knob (Tables~\ref{tab:bvdiam_n20}--\ref{tab:bvdiam_n40},
exact-match by diameter) makes clear that the difficulty tracks those, not the gap. \textbf{The model
fails on the big-dense-clique barbells} (small diameter: two $10$-cliques are dense,
out-of-distribution, and trigger the degree heuristic of Section~\ref{sec:third}) and  \textbf{succeeds
only as the cliques shrink to sparse} $2$--$4$-node ends on a long bridge (large diameter) ---
while the gap is essentially constant across that whole range. So barbell\_var, like
expander\_var below, does not isolate the gap: it just reconfirms that density and the bridge
length matter (Thread~1). (The read-out makes no difference to it either --- linear and
similarity are identical --- Thread~3.)

\subsection{Results for expander\_var: a sweep in the density size}

A natural way to sweep the gap continuously is to vary the degree of a random-regular graph
(\emph{expander\_var}). The catch: a higher degree also means more edges --- a denser graph ---
which is out-of-distribution for a model trained on sparse graphs. Tables~\ref{tab:expvar}--\ref{tab:expvar_n40}
show this per seed. At $n=20$ (Table~\ref{tab:expvar}) the mixed model solves every gap; the ER
model is non-monotone and strongly seed-dependent --- the rise at small gaps is a real gap
effect, but the fall at the largest gaps is the \emph{density} confound (the densest, most
out-of-distribution graphs), not the gap. At $n=40$ (Table~\ref{tab:expvar_n40}) the same shape
is even sharper and now consistent across seeds: the ER model collapses already by gap $0.3$
(rather than $0.6$ at $n=20$), because the $n=40$ model was trained on the sparser
$\mathrm{ER}(40,0.05)$ and tolerates even less density. So at both sizes, varying the degree
changes two things at once and does not isolate the gap.

\subsection{Results for isolation of bottleneck}
\label{sec:ppclean}

The two sweeps above did not isolate the gap because each moved something else with it. So we
built a probe that holds everything else fixed. Two terminals $s,t$ are joined by $k$
internally-disjoint paths of length $\ell$, so the \emph{distance} $\mathrm{dist}(s,t)=\ell$ is
the same for every $k$; the terminals are padded with leaves to a \emph{fixed degree} (so they do
not become higher-degree hubs as $k$ grows); and the rest of the canvas is filled with a sparse
path (so the \emph{density} stays $\approx2$, like the training graphs, with no isolated padding).
The only thing that changes with $k$ is the number of parallel routes between the terminals, i.e.\
the effective resistance $\ell/k$. We measure the accuracy on the $(s,t)$ pair (always connected)
at each $(k,\ell)$, on the standard \emph{linear} models at $n=40$, ER-trained and mixed-trained,
four seeds each (Table~\ref{tab:ppclean}, Figure~\ref{fig:ppclean}).

Now the answer is clean. \emph{Within} capacity ($\ell\le9$) the mixed model gets the pair right
with a single path already, so $k$ barely matters. \emph{Beyond} capacity ($\ell=11,13,15$) a
single path ($k=1$) fails (accuracy $\approx0.55$ --- the distance is past $3^L=9$), but adding
parallel routes rescues it: $k=2$ lifts it to $\approx0.93$ and $k=3$ to $1.00$, at the
\emph{same} distance and density. So the effective resistance genuinely matters, independently of
the distance: with $k$ routes there are $k$ length-$\ell$ walks instead of one, the connection
signal is $k$ times stronger, and the model confirms a connection it could not confirm over a
single path of the same length. (The ER model, which never saw path-like graphs, is noisy and
shows no such structure.) This is the one place we \emph{do} isolate the bottleneck, and the
answer is positive: a tighter bottleneck (fewer routes, higher resistance) is harder, at fixed
distance and density. It does not contradict Thread~1 --- on \emph{natural} graphs the gap is
still collinear with the diameter, so it takes a built probe to separate them --- but it shows
the bottleneck is a real, separable cause, not merely a confounded direction.

\subsection{Conclusion on the spectral gap}
\label{sec:gap-conclusion}

This thread asked one question: does a bottleneck --- a small spectral gap --- make a graph hard
\emph{beyond} what its diameter already explains, and can we measure the gap on its own? The four
experiments above answer it in three steps.

\paragraph{A bottleneck does add difficulty.} The fixed barbell (Table~\ref{tab:bar_n40}) shows it
at the pair level: the model gets the within-clique pairs right but fails exactly the pairs whose
only route crosses the single bridge, and it fails them already at $d=3$ --- far \emph{inside} the
capacity $9$, where every non-bottleneck family is still perfect. A plain path has just as small a
spectral gap and is solved all the way to $d=9$, so what breaks the barbell is specifically the
narrow cut between two dense regions, not the distance.

\paragraph{But on a natural graph the gap cannot be read off on its own.} The two attempts to
grade the effect by sweeping the gap both fail, because each one moves something else along with
it. \emph{barbell\_var} (Table~\ref{tab:bvdiam_n20}) barely changes the gap at all --- a barbell's
gap is tiny and stays tiny across the whole sweep --- while it \emph{does} change the clique
density and the bridge length, and the difficulty follows those, not the gap. \emph{expander\_var}
(Table~\ref{tab:expvar}) raises the gap only by raising the degree, which also makes the graph
denser and therefore out-of-distribution; the failure at large gaps is that density, not the gap.
So neither sweep isolates the gap.

\paragraph{A purpose-built probe isolates it, and the effect is real.} Only the confound-free
parallel-paths probe (Section~\ref{sec:ppclean}) holds the distance and the density fixed and
changes \emph{only} the number of parallel routes between the two terminals --- that is, the
effective resistance. There the answer is clean: beyond the capacity a single route fails
($\approx0.55$) while two or three routes succeed ($0.93$, then $1.00$), at the same distance and
the same density. A tighter bottleneck (fewer routes, higher resistance) is genuinely harder.

\paragraph{Bottom line.} The bottleneck is a real and \emph{separable} cause of difficulty --- but
only once we construct a graph to expose it. On the graphs one meets in the wild it stays tangled
with the diameter and the density, so the spectral gap is not a third axis one can read off a
natural graph.

\section{Thread 3: the read-out --- can a different head push the wall out?}
\label{sec:readout}

Threads~1--2 found the model's main limit is the $3^L$ distance wall, a property of the model
that steering the data cannot move (Report~III). Here we ask whether the \emph{read-out} --- how
the model turns its per-node embeddings $h_i$ into a prediction $\hat R_{ij}$ --- can move it. The
\textbf{standard (linear)} read-out maps the embeddings to $\hat R_{ij}$ through a symmetrised
linear layer, which in practice reads connectivity off essentially a single node's embedding. We
compare it to a \textbf{similarity} read-out that instead \emph{compares} the two embeddings,
\[ \hat R_{ij} = \mathrm{scale}\cdot\cos(h_i, h_j) + \mathrm{bias}, \]
and, separately, to an auxiliary \textbf{Laplacian-smoothness loss}. Both are non-standard, so we
read them as diagnostics (``what is the trunk missing''), not as a recommended architecture. Every
table keeps the read-outs (and where relevant the ER vs mixed training) separate, per seed, as in
Threads~1--2. This thread also collects the two remaining error types --- the \emph{cut} and the
\emph{degree heuristic} --- because both are about what the read-out and the data can or cannot
repair.

\subsection{Two ways to be wrong: under-reaching and over-connecting}
\label{sec:reachcut}

The model can \emph{under-reach} (miss a far-but-connected pair --- the $3^L$ wall) or
\emph{over-connect} (wrongly join two components --- a cut failure). These differ: a clean
matrix-power model has the reach wall but cuts perfectly (separate components share no path
at any length; we verified this with an oracle), so a cut failure means the embeddings leak.
The standard model does both, on the same long families (Table~\ref{tab:reachcut}): the
linear read-out loses cut accuracy on chain\_plus ($0.89$) and path\_union ($0.97$). The
similarity read-out improves reach \emph{and} cut with no trade-off (chain\_plus cut
$0.89\to0.99$); the Laplacian loss does the opposite (it spends cut to buy reach). The
read-out is the better lever. \emph{What kind} of cut error this is --- whether it depends
on how close the two sides were --- is tested in Section~\ref{sec:nearmiss}.

\subsection{A direct test of the cut: the near-miss probe}
\label{sec:nearmiss}

We split a single chain into two components by removing one edge, and measure cut accuracy
on the cross-component pairs vs their \emph{near-miss distance} (how far apart they would be
if the edge returned), with the reach curve on the same axis. A clean matrix-power model
cuts perfectly at every near-miss distance (verified with an oracle), so a flat curve at
$1.0$ means clean matrix powering, while a dip at small near-miss distances would mean the
embedding remembers the almost-path.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \figorbox{runs/report4/report4_figs/near_miss_cut.png}{1.0}
    \caption{Near-miss cut test. Solid $=$ cut accuracy vs near-miss distance; dashed $=$
    reach vs distance, same axis. Left: $n=40$ mixed (mean of four seeds); right: $n=64$
    depth-sweep $L=2$ (one seed). Reach drops sharply at capacity ($9$ linear, $18$
    similarity); the cut is a shallow, broad dip with no step there, and the similarity
    read-out flattens it to $\approx1.0$.}
    \label{fig:nearmiss}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The cut is a mild, broad over-connection, not a second wall.}
(Figure~\ref{fig:nearmiss}.) The linear read-out does not cut perfectly --- its cut accuracy
dips to $\approx0.8$, so the embeddings do leak --- but unlike the reach cliff at $9$ the cut
only sags gently and has no step at $3^L=9$ or $2\cdot 3^L=18$; the dip is worst at
\emph{intermediate} distances (around $7$ at $n=40$, $25$ at $n=64$) and moves with the
model, so it is a model-specific imperfection, not a function of the capacity. The similarity
read-out flattens it to $0.95$--$1.0$ everywhere. So reach and cut are genuinely different
failures, and the similarity read-out cleans up both.

\subsection{The Laplacian-smoothness loss}

A different lever is to add, to the cross-entropy, an auxiliary loss that directly pushes the
embeddings of \emph{adjacent} nodes together:
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{smooth}} \;=\; \frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\lVert h_i-h_j\rVert^2
   \;=\; \frac{1}{|E|}\,\mathrm{Tr}\!\big(H^\top L\, H\big), \]
where $H$ stacks the node embeddings, $L=D-A$ is the graph Laplacian and $|E|$ the number of
edges; the term is weighted by $\lambda$. The motivation is spectral: the kernel of $L$ is exactly
the per-component indicator vectors, so connected nodes ``should'' end up with equal embeddings.

The effect splits by training set (Table~\ref{tab:lambda}, $\lambda$ swept over eight seeds).
Under \textbf{mixed} training it is \emph{inert} ($\lambda=0$ vs $10^{-3}$: $|\Delta|\le0.04$ per
family), because the similarity read-out already drives the smoothness energy to $\approx0$ on
its own. On the \textbf{ER} baseline it does act, as a \emph{connection-vs-cut} lever: a small
$\lambda$ extends long-range reach (chain\_plus $0.11\to0.41$, 1chain $0.64\to0.92$) but blurs
the boundary the model needs to \emph{separate} components, hurting the one family that needs a
cut (2chains $0.47\to0.20$, its cut accuracy $0.99\to0.94$); a large $\lambda=10^{-2}$
over-smooths and collapses everything. So it trades cut for connection rather than removing the
wall, and is redundant once mixed training or the similarity read-out is used; we drop it for the
main results.

\subsection{A third failure mode: the degree heuristic --- fixed only by the data}
\label{sec:third}

Thread~1 found that graphs \emph{denser} than the training set are out-of-distribution and fail
(Section~\ref{sec:density}, Figure~\ref{fig:accdens}). The sharpest single instance of that is the
\textbf{2cliques} family, and it belongs in this thread because it is the one failure the
read-out and the loss \emph{cannot} repair. On 2cliques the model has two jobs: \emph{connect} the
pairs inside each clique (distance $1$, directly adjacent --- the easiest case in the report) and
\emph{cut} the pairs across the two cliques. Table~\ref{tab:degree} splits the accuracy into those
two jobs plus the whole-graph exact-match, for every training/read-out combination of this report
($n=20$, eight seeds), including the Laplacian-loss run.

The standard \textbf{ER, linear} baseline fails even the easy within-clique $d=1$ pairs (reach
$0.71$, and seed-dependent, $0.20$--$1.00$): it mis-classifies \emph{directly connected} nodes
just because they sit in a dense clique where every node has a high degree --- a degree-based
shortcut, learned from sparse ER alone, that the high degree throws off. This is the
\emph{degree heuristic}, and it is the same density-out-of-distribution effect as in Thread~1, now
pinned to one family. The interventions of this thread act on it as follows:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.1em}
  \item the \textbf{similarity read-out} fixes the within-clique reach outright ($\to1.00$) but
        then \emph{over-connects} the two cliques (cut $\to0$), so exact-match stays $0$: never
        having seen a dense block it must split off, it does not know the cliques are separate;
  \item the \textbf{Laplacian loss} changes nothing here (identical to the read-out alone) --- it
        helps long-range \emph{connection}, which is not the problem; 2cliques needs a cut;
  \item only \textbf{mixed training} solves the whole family (reach, cut and exact all $1.00$,
        either read-out), because the mixed set contains dense disconnected blocks (2cliques
        itself, clique\_blocks) and so teaches both to connect inside a clique and to cut between
        cliques.
\end{itemize}
So this failure is repaired only by the right \emph{data}, not by the read-out or the loss ---
the mirror image of the distance wall (which the read-out doubles) and unlike the bottleneck of
Thread~2 (which nothing here touches).

\section{Takeaway}

\emph{Difficulty (Threads~1--2).} A graph is hard for the standard model along two axes we can
separate: \textbf{distance vs.\ capacity} (the $3^L=9$ wall --- the model confirms a connection
only within $\sim9$ hops, sharply, as the per-seed tables show) and \textbf{density vs.\ training}
(graphs denser than the ER training set are out-of-distribution and fail, the $n=40$ model almost
at once). The spectral gap is \emph{not} a third axis one can read off a \emph{natural} graph:
there it is collinear with the diameter ($-0.82$), and the two naive ``gap sweeps''
(barbell\_var, expander\_var) turn out to vary the density and the diameter, not the gap. But a
real bottleneck does add difficulty, and a confound-free probe --- fixed distance, fixed density,
only the number of parallel routes varied --- \emph{isolates} it: beyond the capacity a single
route fails where two or three succeed. So the bottleneck/resistance is a genuine, separable cause
when we construct for it, even though a natural graph confounds it with the diameter.

\emph{Architecture (Thread~3).} The \textbf{similarity read-out} ($\hat R_{ij}\propto\cos(h_i,h_j)$)
doubles the reach radius, from $3^L$ to $2\cdot3^L$ (measured at $L=1,2$ and on the held-out
chain\_plus at $n=40$), because it compares two $3^L$-hop neighbourhoods that meet in the middle;
it also removes the model's over-connection error (the cut), with no trade-off --- but it cannot
cross a bottleneck (the barbell stays at $0$). The \textbf{Laplacian loss} is redundant once the
read-out or mixed training is used; on the plain ER baseline it only trades reach for cut. Both
are non-standard, so we read them as diagnostics, not a recommended architecture. The
\textbf{degree heuristic} (failing the easy within-clique pairs of dense graphs) is repaired at
the pair level by either the read-out or mixed training, but the whole 2cliques family is solved
only by mixed training --- the right \emph{data}, not a different head.
