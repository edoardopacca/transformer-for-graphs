\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Preliminary.} The quantitative results below come from a single
training seed for the new read-out runs (plus the eight reproduction seeds of
Report~III for the baseline). They fix the experimental design and the first
qualitative reading; the numbers will move once the multi-seed and $n=40$ runs,
now training, land. The larger ``parallel-paths'' probe is running on the
existing $n=64$ checkpoints.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This fourth report follows two findings from the earlier ones. The \emph{data
lever} does not help a standard transformer generalise (Report~III, Part~I: the
outcome is seed-dominated), and an $L$-layer model hits a sharp capacity wall at
shortest-path distance $\approx 3^L$ ($=9$ for our $L=2$ models). The wall is a
property of the model, not of the training data, so steering the data cannot move
it. Here we ask whether a \emph{spectral} ingredient can, and, more basically,
whether the diameter is even the right way to measure how hard a graph is. Three
threads:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Does a spectral read-out help?} (Section~\ref{sec:readout}.)
    We replace the standard linear read-out with a \emph{similarity} read-out,
    $\hat R_{ij}\propto\cos(h_i,h_j)$, and optionally add a Laplacian-smoothness
    loss.
    \item \textbf{Is the diameter the right notion of difficulty?}
    (Section~\ref{sec:diff}.) We evaluate on a panel of graph families spanning
    a wide range of structures and ask which property of the graph tracks the
    error.
    \item \textbf{Is the spectral gap (the bottleneck) the bottleneck?}
    (Section~\ref{sec:gap}.) We try to isolate it from confounds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Model and training.} The RoBERTa-faithful standard transformer of
Report~III ($L=2$, single head, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=512$, normalised-ReLU
attention), trained online at $n=20$ for $10^6$ steps. Two training regimes:
\textbf{ER} (Set~A, $\mathrm{ER}(20,0.08)$ only, as in the paper) and
\textbf{mixed} (Set~B, a uniform mixture of the structured families below). Two
read-outs (\emph{linear}, \emph{similarity}) and an optional Laplacian loss.

\paragraph{Two structural quantities.} Throughout we use two ways to summarise a
graph. The \textbf{diameter} is the largest shortest-path distance between any
two nodes (how many hops the farthest pair is apart). The \textbf{spectral gap}
is the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of the normalised Laplacian; intuitively it
measures how fast information mixes through the graph: a large gap means no
bottleneck (a well-connected graph), a small gap means a bottleneck (a long
corridor or a thin bridge).

\subsection{The graph families}
\label{sec:families}

We test on a panel of families chosen to spread out across the diameter and the
spectral gap (Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}):

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.1em}
  \item \textbf{ER}: an Erd\H os--R\'enyi random graph (each edge present with
        probability $p$); the training distribution of Set~A.
  \item \textbf{1chain / 1cycle}: a single path / ring through all nodes --- one
        component, large diameter, tiny gap.
  \item \textbf{2chains / 2cliques / 2cycle}: two disjoint paths / cliques /
        rings --- two components, so the model must also \emph{separate} them.
  \item \textbf{er\_blocks / clique\_blocks}: $1$--$4$ disjoint blocks, each an
        internally-connected ER graph / a complete graph.
  \item \textbf{path\_union}: a disjoint union of $1$--$4$ random paths.
  \item \textbf{barbell}: two cliques joined by a single path --- one component
        but with a hard \emph{bottleneck} (smallest gap of all).
  \item \textbf{expander}: a random regular graph --- very well connected
        (small diameter, large gap), the opposite of a path.
  \item \textbf{chain\_plus}: a long chain plus a small separate component ---
        built to contain within-component distances past $9$ \emph{together with}
        a disconnection, so it exposes the $3^L$ wall even at $n=20$.
  \item \textbf{parallel paths}: two nodes joined by $k$ disjoint paths of equal
        length --- the distance is fixed but the number of paths varies
        (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).
\end{itemize}
\emph{expander} and \emph{barbell} are deliberate opposites and are held out of
the mixed training set, so they probe unseen structure. For each family the
evaluation reports, per node pair, accuracy split by shortest-path distance, and
exact-match accuracy bucketed by diameter and by spectral gap.

\section{Thread 1: the read-out}
\label{sec:readout}

\paragraph{The Laplacian loss is inert.} In an earlier $n=64$ study a large
weight $\lambda=1$ collapsed the model to ``predict everything connected'' (the
smoothness term, $O(d_{\mathrm{model}})$ at init, swamps the cross-entropy). With
a small $\lambda$ and a warm-up, the sweep $\lambda\in\{0,10^{-4},10^{-3},
10^{-2}\}$ gives a clean answer: at convergence $\lambda$ is \emph{inert}. The
Laplacian energy falls to $\approx0$ on its own under the similarity read-out
(making $\cos(h_i,h_j)\!\approx\!1$ for connected nodes already does what the
loss wants), so all $\lambda$ reach the same accuracy. The read-out, not the
loss, is the lever; we drop $\lambda$.

\paragraph{The similarity read-out helps long-range reach, not bottlenecks.}
Table~\ref{tab:simlin} compares the linear and similarity read-outs (mixed
training, one seed). On the families that are about \emph{propagating} a label
over a long distance --- path\_union and, tellingly, the held-out chain\_plus
(genuine out-of-distribution) --- the similarity read-out closes the gap to a
perfect score. On the barbell, whose difficulty is a \emph{bottleneck}, it
changes nothing. This is consistent with the mechanism: the read-out cannot
create mixing capacity the $L=2$ trunk does not have, so a bottleneck must be
attacked on the trunk (more depth), not on the read-out.

\section{Thread 2: the diameter, or also the bottleneck?}
\label{sec:diff}

\emph{This thread is open: the signals below point in two directions, and the
verdict waits on the seeds now training.} Table~\ref{tab:fam} reports the baseline
(ER, linear) on the family panel, averaged over the eight reproduction seeds, with
the typical diameter and gap of each family. Sorted by gap, a pattern appears that
the diameter \emph{alone} does not explain --- though, as chain\_plus shows below,
the diameter still captures a real effect (the $3^L$ wall). The two readings are
not in conflict so much as incomplete; the question is whether the gap adds to the
diameter, not whether it replaces it.

by gap.}
\label{tab:fam}

The suggestive comparison is \emph{barbell vs.\ 1chain}: the barbell has the
\emph{smaller} diameter ($11$ vs.\ $19$) yet is \emph{harder} ($0.22$ vs.\
$0.45$) --- on this pair the diameter and the difficulty disagree, while the gap
agrees. The expander points the same way: smallest diameter, largest gap,
essentially solved, and never seen in training. Figure~\ref{fig:diamgap} pools all
families: accuracy vs diameter is non-monotone (a dip around the capacity, a
recovery further out --- the wall, see below), while vs gap the sharp drop is at
the smallest gaps. We read this as a hint that the gap carries information the
diameter misses, \emph{not} as evidence against the diameter; both may matter, and
disentangling them is exactly what the controlled probe of Section~\ref{sec:gap}
is for.

\paragraph{chain\_plus makes the $3^L$ wall visible at $n=20$ --- distance does
matter.} The wall was hard to see at $n=20$ because the disconnected families have
components too small to hold within-distances beyond $9$ (a $2$-chain of $k=10$
tops out at $d=9$). The purpose-built chain\_plus does hold them. Its reach by
distance (seed $8000$) is exactly the Report-III signature: perfect to $9$, a
crash just beyond, a partial recovery further out. This is the clearest evidence
that the diameter / distance \emph{does} capture a real effect --- which is why we
frame this thread as ``diameter \emph{and} gap'', not ``gap instead of diameter''.

\section{Thread 3: the spectral gap, and a confound}
\label{sec:gap}

\paragraph{An honest negative on the naive test.} To vary the gap continuously we
evaluate \emph{expander\_var} (a random regular graph of varying degree, hence
varying gap), single-component, so it is pure reach. The accuracy by gap is
\emph{not} monotone:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
gap & $0.04$--$0.08$ & $0.08$--$0.15$ & $0.15$--$0.30$ & $0.30$--$0.60$ & $0.60$--$1.0$ \\
\midrule
exact & 0.59 & 0.98 & 0.99 & 0.80 & 0.51 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The reason is a confound we did not anticipate: increasing the degree raises the
gap \emph{but also the density}, and a high-degree graph has far more edges than
the sparse $\mathrm{ER}(20,0.08)$ the model was trained on --- it is
\emph{out-of-distribution in density}, not hard because of the gap. So
expander\_var does not isolate the gap (it varies two things at once); the
rising part (gap $0.04\!\to\!0.15$) is genuine, the falling part is the confound.

\paragraph{The clean test: distance fixed, paths varied.} To isolate the gap from
both distance and density we use \emph{parallel paths}: two terminals joined by
$k$ disjoint paths of length $\ell$. The distance is $\ell$ for \emph{every} $k$,
but the effective resistance (how hard it is to push information across, treating
each edge as a unit resistor) is $\ell/k$, falling with $k$. We vary $k$ at fixed
$\ell$ and read the error on the terminal pair. The predictions diverge: if
difficulty is \emph{distance} (the paper) the error is flat in $k$; if it is
\emph{resistance / bottleneck} (our reading) it falls with $k$. We also record
the model's true end-to-end influence $\|\partial h_t/\partial x_s\|$. The
contrast is sharpest \emph{past} the capacity $9$, which needs room, so this runs
on the $n=64$ reach checkpoints (and $n=40$), not at $n=20$.

\section{A third failure mode: the degree heuristic}
\label{sec:third}

Separating connected (``reach'') from disconnected (``cut'') pairs is useful but
does not explain everything. On 2cliques the reach accuracy is only $0.71$ ---
and these are pairs at distance $1$, directly adjacent inside a clique. The model
mis-classifies \emph{directly connected} pairs in a dense graph: the signature of
the \textbf{degree heuristic} (every node in a clique has high degree, and the
model is misled), not a reach or a cut limitation. There are thus three ways the
model fails: it cannot reach across a bottleneck, it sometimes cannot cut, and on
dense graphs it falls back on degree.

\section{Takeaway}

On one seed, and with everything still in flight: the Laplacian loss is redundant
with the similarity read-out and is dropped; the similarity read-out helps
\emph{long-range reach} (even out-of-distribution) but not bottlenecks, which
points the architectural search at the trunk rather than the read-out. On
difficulty the picture is genuinely open: the $3^L$ wall is real and tied to
distance (chain\_plus), yet a few comparisons (barbell vs.\ chain, the expander)
hint that the spectral gap / bottleneck adds something the diameter misses --- so
the working hypothesis is \emph{diameter and gap}, not one or the other, and the
naive gap sweep is confounded by density. Finally, beyond reach and cut there is a
third, degree-based failure mode on dense graphs. The multi-seed, $n=40$ and
parallel-paths runs now training will tell us which of these survive.
