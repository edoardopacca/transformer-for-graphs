\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Preliminary.} The headline result --- that a similarity read-out
moves the distance wall from $3^L$ outward --- is now reproduced across
\textbf{two independent $n=40$ seeds}, near-identical digit for digit, so it is no
longer single-seed; the remaining $n=20$ seeds and a third $n=40$ seed are still
training. The depth sweep \emph{with} the similarity read-out has finished and pins the
mechanism: the read-out \textbf{doubles} the reach radius from $3^L$ to
$2\cdot 3^L$ (a constant factor, the wall still scaling with depth), measured at
$L=1$ and $L=2$. The controlled ``parallel-paths'' gap probe, by contrast, came
back \textbf{inconclusive} (a distribution-shift confound, Section~\ref{sec:gap});
the graded gap evidence rests instead on barbell\_var. Numbers will still move at
the margins (a third $n=40$ seed and the remaining $n=20$ seeds are training), but
the design and the qualitative reading are fixed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This fourth report follows two findings from the earlier ones. The \emph{data
lever} does not help a standard transformer generalise (Report~III, Part~I: the
outcome is seed-dominated), and an $L$-layer model hits a sharp capacity wall at
shortest-path distance $\approx 3^L$ ($=9$ for our $L=2$ models). The wall is a
property of the model, not of the training data, so steering the data cannot move
it. Here we ask whether a \emph{spectral} ingredient can, and, more basically,
whether the diameter is even the right way to measure how hard a graph is. Three
threads:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Does a spectral read-out help?} (Section~\ref{sec:readout}.)
    We replace the standard linear read-out with a \emph{similarity} read-out,
    $\hat R_{ij}\propto\cos(h_i,h_j)$, and optionally add a Laplacian-smoothness
    loss.
    \item \textbf{Is the diameter the right notion of difficulty?}
    (Section~\ref{sec:diff}.) We evaluate on a panel of graph families spanning
    a wide range of structures and ask which property of the graph tracks the
    error.
    \item \textbf{Is the spectral gap (the bottleneck) the bottleneck?}
    (Section~\ref{sec:gap}.) We try to isolate it from confounds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Model and training.} The RoBERTa-faithful standard transformer of
Report~III ($L=2$, single head, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=512$, normalised-ReLU
attention), trained online at $n=20$ for $10^6$ steps. Two training regimes:
\textbf{ER} (Set~A, $\mathrm{ER}(20,0.08)$ only, as in the paper) and
\textbf{mixed} (Set~B, a uniform mixture of the structured families below). Two
read-outs (\emph{linear}, \emph{similarity}) and an optional Laplacian loss.

\paragraph{Two structural quantities.} Throughout we use two ways to summarise a
graph. The \textbf{diameter} is the largest shortest-path distance between any
two nodes (how many hops the farthest pair is apart). The \textbf{spectral gap}
is the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of the normalised Laplacian; intuitively it
measures how fast information mixes through the graph: a large gap means no
bottleneck (a well-connected graph), a small gap means a bottleneck (a long
corridor or a thin bridge).

\subsection{The graph families}
\label{sec:families}

We test on a panel of families chosen to spread out across the diameter and the
spectral gap (Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}):

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.1em}
  \item \textbf{ER}: an Erd\H os--R\'enyi random graph (each edge present with
        probability $p$); the training distribution of Set~A.
  \item \textbf{1chain / 1cycle}: a single path / ring through all nodes --- one
        component, large diameter, tiny gap.
  \item \textbf{2chains / 2cliques / 2cycle}: two disjoint paths / cliques /
        rings --- two components, so the model must also \emph{separate} them.
  \item \textbf{er\_blocks / clique\_blocks}: $1$--$4$ disjoint blocks, each an
        internally-connected ER graph / a complete graph.
  \item \textbf{path\_union}: a disjoint union of $1$--$4$ random paths.
  \item \textbf{barbell}: two cliques joined by a single path --- one component
        but with a hard \emph{bottleneck} (smallest gap of all).
  \item \textbf{expander}: a \emph{random regular} graph --- every node has the
        same small number of neighbours (here $3$), and those neighbours are
        chosen at random rather than laid out in a line. Because the wiring is
        random, from any node you reach the whole graph in very few hops: there
        is no thin bridge and no long corridor to traverse, so the diameter is
        tiny and the spectral gap is large. It is the structural opposite of a
        path (one long corridor), and we hold it out of training to test whether
        the model copes with a well-connected graph it has never seen.
  \item \textbf{chain\_plus}: a long chain plus a small separate component ---
        built to contain within-component distances past $9$ \emph{together with}
        a disconnection, so it exposes the $3^L$ wall even at $n=20$.
  \item \textbf{parallel paths}: two nodes joined by $k$ disjoint paths of equal
        length --- the distance is fixed but the number of paths varies
        (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).
\end{itemize}
\emph{expander} and \emph{barbell} are deliberate opposites and are held out of
the mixed training set, so they probe unseen structure. For each family the
evaluation reports, per node pair, accuracy split by shortest-path distance, and
exact-match accuracy bucketed by diameter and by spectral gap.

\section{Thread 1: the read-out}
\label{sec:readout}

This thread belongs to the second of our two questions --- not ``what makes a
graph hard'' (Sections~\ref{sec:diff}--\ref{sec:gap}) but ``can we change the
architecture to handle what we found hard''. The similarity read-out is a
deliberate \emph{intervention}: it is non-standard, so its behaviour is only
interesting as an answer to ``does this move the wall we identified''. We
therefore read its numbers as a before/after on the difficulties of the next
sections, not as difficulty results in their own right.

\paragraph{The Laplacian loss is inert.} In an earlier $n=64$ study a large
weight $\lambda=1$ collapsed the model to ``predict everything connected'' (the
smoothness term, $O(d_{\mathrm{model}})$ at init, swamps the cross-entropy). With
a small $\lambda$ and a warm-up, the sweep $\lambda\in\{0,10^{-4},10^{-3},
10^{-2}\}$ gives a clean answer: at convergence $\lambda$ is \emph{inert}. The
Laplacian energy falls to $\approx0$ on its own under the similarity read-out
(making $\cos(h_i,h_j)\!\approx\!1$ for connected nodes already does what the
loss wants), so all $\lambda$ reach the same accuracy. The read-out, not the
loss, is the lever; we drop $\lambda$.

\paragraph{The similarity read-out helps long-range reach, not bottlenecks.}
Table~\ref{tab:simlin} compares the linear and similarity read-outs (mixed
training, one seed). On the families that are about \emph{propagating} a label
over a long distance --- path\_union and, tellingly, the held-out chain\_plus
(genuine out-of-distribution) --- the similarity read-out closes the gap to a
perfect score. On the barbell, whose difficulty is a \emph{bottleneck}, it
changes nothing.

\paragraph{At $n=40$ we can see \emph{how} the read-out helps, and it is more
than we first thought.} At $n=20$ the structured families are too small to hold
within-component distances past $9$, so the exact-match numbers above hide the
mechanism. The $n=40$ runs --- now \textbf{two independent seeds} --- open the
distance range up. Table~\ref{tab:chainplus_n40} traces reach by distance on the
held-out chain\_plus (a genuine out-of-distribution graph), for both read-outs
and both seeds. The standard \emph{linear} read-out breaks exactly at the
matrix-powering capacity $3^L=9$, then sits at a $\approx0.55$ floor through the
whole mid-range. The \emph{similarity} read-out, on the very same $L=2$ trunk,
stays perfect far past the wall (to $d\approx18$) before a shallow dip, and on the
farthest pairs recovers to $1.00$. The two seeds are near-identical digit for
digit, so this is not a lucky initialisation: the read-out genuinely moves the
distance wall, out-of-distribution included. This revises our first reading. The
linear read-out's clean break at $3^L$ is partly an \emph{artefact of forcing a
local, matrix-power reading} onto the embedding; a global (cosine-similarity)
reading of the same embedding reaches much farther. What the read-out cannot do
is cross a bottleneck --- the barbell stays at $0.00$ for both read-outs and both
seeds (Section~\ref{sec:diff}) --- and that is the genuine limit of the $L=2$
trunk, to be attacked with depth, not with the read-out.

\paragraph{The mechanism: the read-out \emph{doubles} the reach radius, it does
not change the regime.} The wall-moving above raises a sharp question: does the
similarity read-out lift the distance limit by a constant factor, or remove its
dependence on depth altogether? We answer it by repeating the depth sweep of
Report~III (train at $n=64$ on the path-union distribution, vary only the number
of layers $L$) \emph{with the similarity read-out}, and comparing the reach
profile to the linear sweep. Table~\ref{tab:depthsim} is unambiguous. The linear
read-out resolves pairs up to exactly $3^L$ ($d=3$ for $L=1$, $d=9$ for $L=2$),
the matrix-powering capacity. The similarity read-out, on the identical trunk,
resolves up to exactly \emph{twice} that --- $d=6$ for $L=1$, $d=18$ for $L=2$ ---
and then degrades, with the same boundary recovery on the farthest pairs. So the
read-out buys a clean factor of $2$, and the wall \emph{still scales with depth}
($\times 3$ per layer): it multiplies the capacity, it does not abolish the
distance dependence. (At $L=3$ both read-outs are perfect across the whole $n=64$
range, $\ge 2\cdot 3^3 = 54$, so this $n$ cannot separate them --- a larger $n$
would be needed.)

This has a simple reading. The linear read-out infers $R_{ij}$ from a single
node embedding $h_i$, which carries a $\sim 3^L$-hop ``light cone'' of
propagation --- hence reach $3^L$. The similarity read-out compares \emph{two}
embeddings $\cos(h_i,h_j)$, each carrying its own $3^L$-hop cone, so the pair is
resolved as soon as the two cones overlap: a meet-in-the-middle at distance
$\sim 2\cdot 3^L$. Strikingly, the $d=18$ wall measured here at $n=64$ is the same
wall seen independently on the $n=40$ chain\_plus family
(Table~\ref{tab:chainplus_n40}) --- two unrelated experiments agreeing on the
constant. The doubling also shows in-distribution: at $L=2$ the similarity
read-out lifts exact match on the held-out path-union test from $0.40$ to $0.62$.

\section{Thread 2: the diameter, or also the bottleneck?}
\label{sec:diff}

\subsection{The difficulty map (evaluation running)}
\label{sec:diffmap}

\paragraph{Purpose.} This is the question the project was originally set to answer:
\emph{how does the standard model's accuracy vary with the graph's diameter, and
with its spectral gap, and which of the two is the operative variable?} The
family-by-family numbers below (Table~\ref{tab:fam}) hint at an answer but are
unsatisfying, because each family is a single, isolated point in the (diameter,
gap) plane --- a chain is one corner, an expander another --- so one cannot read
off a clean dependence, let alone separate the two axes. To do that properly we
need (i) graphs that \emph{spread continuously} across the plane and (ii) the
\emph{joint} per-graph record of diameter, gap and accuracy together, which the
per-family evaluation discards.

\paragraph{Method.} We therefore evaluate the standard (linear read-out) model on
a purpose-built pool of single-component graphs that spans the plane: an
Erd\H os--R\'enyi density sweep (the main arc, dense/small-diameter/large-gap down
to sparse/large-diameter/small-gap), a random-regular degree sweep (which moves
the gap at a roughly fixed diameter band --- degree $2$ is a cycle, high degree is
near-complete), a barbell clique-size sweep (the small-gap bottleneck corner), and
single chains and cycles (the large-diameter corner). For \emph{every} graph we
log its diameter, its spectral gap, its mean degree (a density control), and the
model's exact-match and pairwise accuracy. From this we will build the two things
the question actually needs: a \textbf{two-dimensional accuracy map} over
(diameter, gap), and the \textbf{partial-dependence} curves --- accuracy versus
diameter at a fixed gap, and accuracy versus gap at a fixed diameter --- which are
what disentangle the two variables (and, by conditioning on mean degree, separate
both from a mere density effect). The evaluation is run on the standard linear
checkpoints at $n=20$ (eight reproduction seeds) and $n=40$ (the ER-trained
family seeds), so the map is reported with a seed band, not a single draw.

\paragraph{Status.} The evaluation is running; the per-graph data and the
aggregate figures are not yet in. We state the design here so the reading is fixed
before the numbers land. Our prior, from the piecemeal evidence in the rest of
this thread, is that the map will show accuracy falling along \emph{both} axes ---
a wall in diameter near $3^L$ and a separate degradation at small gap --- with the
partial-dependence at fixed diameter still showing a gap effect; that is the
concrete, falsifiable version of the ``diameter \emph{and} a bottleneck term''
reading.

verdict waits on the seeds now training.} Table~\ref{tab:fam} reports the baseline

\paragraph{A bottleneck bites \emph{inside} the capacity --- so distance is not
the whole story.} chain\_plus shows the distance wall is real; the barbell shows
distance is not enough to explain difficulty. Table~\ref{tab:barbell_d} traces
the standard (linear) model's reach on the barbell by distance, at $n=40$. Every
path/chain/cycle family is at $1.00$ for every $d\le9$ (within capacity, reach is
trivial). The barbell is one connected component, yet its accuracy has already
collapsed by $d=3$ ($0.38$) --- a distance \emph{four times within} the $3^L=9$
budget. The pairs that fail are the ones whose only route runs through the thin
bridge between the two cliques: same model, same read-out, same short distance as
a trivial path pair, but a bottleneck in the way. So within the capacity budget,
where distance predicts ``easy'', a structural bottleneck still makes the pair
hard. We are careful here: the barbell and a long path \emph{both} have a small
spectral gap, yet the path is solved to $d=9$ and the barbell is not, so the
scalar gap is not by itself a clean predictor --- what the barbell adds is a true
\emph{cut} (a single bridge carrying all the flow). Quantifying that cut cleanly
is harder than it looks: our controlled attempt (the parallel-paths probe,
Section~\ref{sec:gap}) came back inconclusive, and the surviving graded evidence
is the \emph{barbell\_var} sweep reported there. The reading stays ``diameter
\emph{and} a bottleneck term'', with the bottleneck term real and monotone but not
yet pinned to a single isolated number.

\section{Thread 3: the spectral gap, and a confound}
\label{sec:gap}

\paragraph{An honest negative on the naive test.} To vary the gap continuously we
evaluate \emph{expander\_var} (a random regular graph of varying degree, hence
varying gap), single-component, so it is pure reach. The accuracy by gap is
\emph{not} monotone:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
gap & $0.04$--$0.08$ & $0.08$--$0.15$ & $0.15$--$0.30$ & $0.30$--$0.60$ & $0.60$--$1.0$ \\
\midrule
exact & 0.59 & 0.98 & 0.99 & 0.80 & 0.51 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The reason is a confound we did not anticipate: increasing the degree raises the
gap \emph{but also the density}, and a high-degree graph has far more edges than
the sparse $\mathrm{ER}(20,0.08)$ the model was trained on --- it is
\emph{out-of-distribution in density}, not hard because of the gap. So
expander\_var does not isolate the gap (it varies two things at once); the
rising part (gap $0.04\!\to\!0.15$) is genuine, the falling part is the confound.

\paragraph{The intended clean test: distance fixed, paths varied.} To isolate the
gap from both distance and density we designed \emph{parallel paths}: two
terminals joined by $k$ disjoint paths of length $\ell$. The distance is $\ell$
for \emph{every} $k$, but the effective resistance (how hard it is to push
information across, treating each edge as a unit resistor) is $\ell/k$, falling
with $k$. Varying $k$ at fixed $\ell$ should separate the two hypotheses: if
difficulty is \emph{distance} (the paper) the terminal error is flat in $k$; if it
is \emph{resistance / bottleneck} (our reading) it falls with $k$. We ran this on
the $n=64$ reach checkpoints ($L=1,2,3$), recording both the terminal accuracy and
the model's true end-to-end influence $\|\partial h_t/\partial x_s\|$.

\paragraph{The probe is inconclusive: a distribution-shift confound.} The result
does not separate the hypotheses, because the construction introduces a confound
of its own. The terminal accuracy is \emph{non-monotone} in $k$ (it often
\emph{collapses} for $k\ge3$, the opposite of the resistance prediction), and the
influence spikes erratically. The tell is the $k=1$ column --- a single path, the
purest in-distribution case --- in Table~\ref{tab:parpaths}: at $L=2$ a single
path of length $\ell=5$, a distance \emph{well within} the capacity $9$, scores
only $0.15$, when the same $L=2$ model resolves $d=5$ perfectly on every
in-distribution family. The reason is that a parallel-paths graph is a handful of
active nodes ($2+k(\ell-1)$) floating in a $64$-node canvas of isolated padding,
with terminals of degree $k$ --- structurally unlike the path-union training
(which fills all $64$ nodes with degree-$\le2$ paths). Raising $k$ makes the
terminals higher-degree hubs, pushing the graph further out of distribution. So
parallel paths varies resistance \emph{and} distribution at once, exactly the kind
of confound we flagged for expander\_var; it does not isolate the gap. A clean
redesign (fill the canvas, hold the terminal degree fixed, or measure resistance
on in-distribution graphs) is needed.

\paragraph{What \emph{does} survive: a graded bottleneck (barbell\_var).} The gap
signal is nonetheless real, and visible in a construction that stays inside one
topology. \emph{barbell\_var} varies the strength of the bottleneck (bridge and
clique sizes) while keeping the barbell shape, so increasing the gap loosens the
cut. Sorted by gap (Table~\ref{tab:barbellvar}), the difficulty falls
monotonically with the gap, and --- importantly --- the linear and similarity
read-outs behave \emph{identically}, confirming that the read-out (which moves the
distance wall) does nothing for a bottleneck. This is the graded, in-topology
version of the within-capacity barbell collapse of Section~\ref{sec:diff}.

\paragraph{Where this leaves the difficulty question.} Three different
constructions now point the same way: the barbell fails inside the capacity
(Section~\ref{sec:diff}), barbell\_var gets monotonically easier as the gap
widens, and expander\_var fails only at the smallest gaps. A small gap (a
bottleneck) makes a pair hard over and above its distance. But no single probe
isolates the gap from everything else --- expander\_var co-varies it with density,
barbell\_var with bridge geometry, and parallel paths with distribution --- so we
report a robust \emph{direction} (gap matters, monotonically) without yet a clean
scalar law. The honest statement remains ``diameter \emph{and} a bottleneck
term''.

\section{A third failure mode: the degree heuristic}
\label{sec:third}

Separating connected (``reach'') from disconnected (``cut'') pairs is useful but
does not explain everything. On 2cliques the reach accuracy is only $0.71$ ---
and these are pairs at distance $1$, directly adjacent inside a clique. The model
mis-classifies \emph{directly connected} pairs in a dense graph: the signature of
the \textbf{degree heuristic} (every node in a clique has high degree, and the
model is misled), not a reach or a cut limitation. There are thus three ways the
model fails: it cannot reach across a bottleneck, it sometimes cannot cut, and on
dense graphs it falls back on degree.

Unlike the bottleneck, this third mode is \emph{recoverable}. At $n=40$ the
ER-trained linear model gets only $0.30$ on the $d=1$ clique pairs of 2cliques
(exact match $0$), but either ingredient removes the failure: training on the
mixture (which contains dense blocks) \emph{or} swapping in the similarity
read-out lifts 2cliques to exact match $1.00$. The degree heuristic is therefore
a property of the narrow ER-only, linear-read-out baseline, not an intrinsic
limit of the architecture --- in contrast to the barbell bottleneck, which
survives both interventions.

\section{What is running}
\label{sec:status}

The substantive runs are in. Two $n=40$ seeds ($1000$, $2000$, all four arms
$\{$ER, mixed$\}\times\{$linear, similarity$\}$) feed the per-distance tables
(\ref{tab:chainplus_n40}, \ref{tab:barbell_d}) and agree almost digit for digit;
the depth sweep with the similarity read-out (Table~\ref{tab:depthsim}) has
finished and pins the mechanism to a factor of $2$; the parallel-paths probe ran
and was inconclusive (Section~\ref{sec:gap}). Still in flight, and only to firm up
margins: (i) a \emph{third} $n=40$ seed ($3000$), for a clean three-seed
statement; (ii) the remaining $n=20$ seeds, of which $2000$--$6000$ are in and
reproduce the direction (similarity $\ge$ linear on the long-range families,
barbell $=0$ for both). Two clean follow-ups remain open: a depth sweep at
$n>64$ to separate the read-outs at $L=3$ (where $n=64$ saturates), and a
confound-free re-run of the parallel-paths probe (fill the canvas, fix the
terminal degree) to isolate the gap.

\section{Takeaway}

With two $n=40$ seeds and the depth sweep in, the two questions sharpen as
follows.

\emph{On the architecture (Thread~1).} The Laplacian loss is redundant with the
similarity read-out and is dropped. The similarity read-out does more than we
first credited it with, and we now know exactly how much: it \emph{doubles} the
reach radius, from the matrix-powering $3^L$ to $2\cdot 3^L$ (measured at $L=1$
and $L=2$, on the identical trunk; Table~\ref{tab:depthsim}), reproducibly across
two $n=40$ seeds. The wall still scales with depth, so this is a clean
constant-factor gain --- the read-out reads connectivity by comparing two
$3^L$-radius embeddings that meet in the middle, rather than one --- not a removal
of the distance dependence. The one thing the read-out cannot fix is the
bottleneck (the
barbell stays at $0$ for both read-outs and both seeds), which is where depth, not
the read-out, will have to do the work. The depth sweep with the similarity
read-out, now running, will say whether its wall scales with $L$ (a constant
factor) or is fixed (a change of regime).

\emph{On difficulty (Threads~2--3).} The $3^L$ distance wall is real (chain\_plus,
visible over a wide range with the linear read-out). But it is not the whole
story: at $n=40$ the barbell fails at $d=3$, four times \emph{inside} the
capacity, where every non-bottleneck family is trivial --- so a structural
bottleneck adds difficulty the distance cannot see. The gap signal is robust in
\emph{direction}: across three constructions (barbell within capacity,
barbell\_var graded by gap, expander\_var) a smaller gap is monotonically harder.
What we do \emph{not} have is a clean isolation: each construction co-varies the
gap with something else (density, bridge geometry, distribution), and our purpose-
built isolator --- the parallel-paths probe --- came back inconclusive, dominated
by a distribution-shift confound rather than by resistance. So we report the
direction, not a scalar law. The third, degree-based failure mode turns out to be
recoverable (mixed training or the similarity read-out removes it), unlike the
bottleneck. The working picture stays \emph{diameter and a bottleneck term}, the
bottleneck term real and monotone but not yet cleanly isolated.
