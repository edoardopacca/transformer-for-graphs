\title{Empirical Analysis of Transformers on Graph Connectivity \\
       \large Part IV: A Similarity Read-out, and What Makes a Graph Hard}
\author{Edoardo Paccagnella}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent This is the fourth report on a standard transformer trained to predict
graph connectivity. It has two goals. First, to understand what makes a graph hard
for the standard model (the one with a plain linear read-out): is it the diameter,
the spectral gap, or something else? Second, to check whether a different read-out
or different training data gives a better model. The main family-level numbers are
now averaged over several seeds (four at $n=40$, eight at $n=20$); two experiments
on a separate set of $n=64$ models --- the depth sweep and the parallel-paths probe
--- are still single-seed and flagged as such.
On difficulty, a ``difficulty map'' built from many graphs shows two axes that can
be told apart: the distance-versus-capacity wall at $3^L=9$, and how dense a graph
is relative to the training set (graphs denser than training are
out-of-distribution and fail). The spectral gap does not come out as a separate
axis: on sparse graphs it is essentially a stand-in for the diameter, and the one
structure that breaks that link (the barbell bottleneck) is tangled up with
density.
On architecture, a similarity read-out ($\hat R_{ij}\propto\cos(h_i,h_j)$) doubles
the reach radius from $3^L$ to $2\cdot 3^L$, but does nothing for a bottleneck. A
Laplacian-smoothness loss is redundant once we use that read-out or the mixed
training set, though on the plain ER baseline it does have an effect (it trades
long-range reach for the ability to separate components). We also correct one
earlier reading that was based on a single seed: a similarity read-out \emph{alone}
does not solve the two-cliques family exactly; only mixed training does.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This fourth report follows two findings from the earlier ones. The \emph{data
lever} does not help a standard transformer generalise (Report~III, Part~I: the
outcome is seed-dominated), and an $L$-layer model hits a sharp capacity wall at
shortest-path distance $\approx 3^L$ ($=9$ for our $L=2$ models). The wall is a
property of the model, not of the training data, so steering the data cannot move
it. Here we ask whether a \emph{spectral} ingredient can, and, more basically,
whether the diameter is even the right way to measure how hard a graph is. Three
threads:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Does a spectral read-out help?} (Section~\ref{sec:readout}.)
    We replace the standard linear read-out with a \emph{similarity} read-out,
    $\hat R_{ij}\propto\cos(h_i,h_j)$, and optionally add a Laplacian-smoothness
    loss.
    \item \textbf{Is the diameter the right notion of difficulty?}
    (Section~\ref{sec:diff}.) We evaluate on a panel of graph families spanning
    a wide range of structures and ask which property of the graph tracks the
    error.
    \item \textbf{Is the spectral gap (the bottleneck) the bottleneck?}
    (Section~\ref{sec:gap}.) We try to isolate it from confounds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Model and training.} The RoBERTa-faithful standard transformer of
Report~III ($L=2$, single head, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=512$, normalised-ReLU
attention), trained online at $n=20$ for $10^6$ steps. Two training regimes:
\textbf{ER} (Set~A, $\mathrm{ER}(20,0.08)$ only, as in the paper) and
\textbf{mixed} (Set~B, a uniform mixture of the structured families below). Two
read-outs (\emph{linear}, \emph{similarity}) and an optional Laplacian loss.

\paragraph{Two structural quantities.} Throughout we use two ways to summarise a
graph. The \textbf{diameter} is the largest shortest-path distance between any
two nodes (how many hops the farthest pair is apart). The \textbf{spectral gap}
is the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of the normalised Laplacian; intuitively it
measures how fast information mixes through the graph: a large gap means no
bottleneck (a well-connected graph), a small gap means a bottleneck (a long
corridor or a thin bridge).

\subsection{The graph families}
\label{sec:families}

We test on a panel of families chosen to spread out across the diameter and the
spectral gap (Figure~\ref{fig:gallery}):

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.1em}
  \item \textbf{ER}: an Erd\H os--R\'enyi random graph (each edge present with
        probability $p$); the training distribution of Set~A.
  \item \textbf{1chain / 1cycle}: a single path / ring through all nodes --- one
        component, large diameter, tiny gap.
  \item \textbf{2chains / 2cliques / 2cycle}: two disjoint paths / cliques /
        rings --- two components, so the model must also \emph{separate} them.
  \item \textbf{er\_blocks / clique\_blocks}: $1$--$4$ disjoint blocks, each an
        internally-connected ER graph / a complete graph.
  \item \textbf{path\_union}: a disjoint union of $1$--$4$ random paths.
  \item \textbf{barbell}: two cliques joined by a single path --- one component
        but with a hard \emph{bottleneck} (smallest gap of all).
  \item \textbf{expander}: a \emph{random regular} graph --- every node has the
        same small number of neighbours (here $3$), and those neighbours are
        chosen at random rather than laid out in a line. Because the wiring is
        random, from any node you reach the whole graph in very few hops: there
        is no thin bridge and no long corridor to traverse, so the diameter is
        tiny and the spectral gap is large. It is the structural opposite of a
        path (one long corridor), and we hold it out of training to test whether
        the model copes with a well-connected graph it has never seen.
  \item \textbf{chain\_plus}: a long chain plus a small separate component ---
        built to contain within-component distances past $9$ \emph{together with}
        a disconnection, so it exposes the $3^L$ wall even at $n=20$.
  \item \textbf{parallel paths}: two nodes joined by $k$ disjoint paths of equal
        length --- the distance is fixed but the number of paths varies
        (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).
\end{itemize}
\emph{expander} and \emph{barbell} are deliberate opposites and are held out of
the mixed training set, so they probe unseen structure. For each family the
evaluation reports, per node pair, accuracy split by shortest-path distance, and
exact-match accuracy bucketed by diameter and by spectral gap.

\section{Thread 1: the read-out}
\label{sec:readout}

This thread is about the second question: can we change the model to do better on
the hard cases? The similarity read-out is a non-standard choice, so we treat it as
a diagnostic --- a before/after on the difficulties found in the next sections
(\ref{sec:diff}--\ref{sec:gap}) --- not as an architecture we are recommending.

\paragraph{The Laplacian loss is redundant under mixed training, but a real lever
on the ER baseline.} In an earlier $n=64$ study a large weight $\lambda=1$
collapsed the model to ``predict everything connected'' (the smoothness term,
$O(d_{\mathrm{model}})$ at init, swamps the cross-entropy). With a small $\lambda$
and a warm-up we swept $\lambda\in\{0,10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}\}$, now over all eight
seeds, and the answer \emph{splits by training distribution}. Under the
\textbf{mixed} training used for the rest of this report the loss is \emph{inert}:
$\lambda=0$ and $\lambda=10^{-3}$ give the same exact-match on every family
(per-family $|\Delta|\le0.04$, within seed noise), because the similarity read-out
already drives the Laplacian energy to $\approx0$ on its own --- making
$\cos(h_i,h_j)\!\approx\!1$ for connected nodes is exactly what the loss wants ---
so it has nothing left to do. On the narrower \textbf{ER-only} baseline, where the
energy is \emph{not} yet at $0$, the loss does act, and it is a
\emph{connection-vs-cut} lever (Table~\ref{tab:lambda}). A small $\lambda$ extends
long-range reach: on the held-out chain\_plus, exact match rises $0.11\to0.41$ and
the mid-range reach floor lifts from $\approx0.48$ to $\approx0.80$; 1chain rises
$0.64\to0.92$. But it does this by pulling connected embeddings together, which
blurs the boundary the model needs to \emph{separate} components: 2chains drops
$0.47\to0.20$ (its cut accuracy $0.99\to0.94$). Too large a $\lambda$ ($10^{-2}$)
over-smooths and collapses the long families outright. So the loss is not a no-op
--- it trades cut for connection --- but it is redundant with the mixture, and we
drop it for the main results, keeping the read-out as the clean lever.

perfect score. On the barbell, whose difficulty is a \emph{bottleneck}, it

\paragraph{At $n=40$ we can see \emph{how} the read-out helps.} At $n=20$ the
structured families are too small to hold within-component distances past $9$, so
the exact-match numbers above hide what is going on. The $n=40$ runs (four seeds)
open the distance range up. Table~\ref{tab:chainplus_n40} and
Figure~\ref{fig:chainplus_n40} trace reach by distance on the held-out chain\_plus
(a graph the model never saw in training), for both read-outs and all four seeds.
The linear read-out breaks at the capacity $3^L=9$ and then sits at a $\approx0.55$
plateau through the middle distances. The similarity read-out, on the same $L=2$
model, stays perfect up to about $d=18$ before a shallow dip, and recovers to
$1.00$ on the farthest pairs. Seeds $1000$--$3000$ are almost identical and seed
$4000$ is a little weaker (perfect to about $d=14$ instead of $18$), but in every
seed the similarity read-out reaches much farther than the linear one, so this is
not one lucky initialisation. A way to read this: the linear read-out infers
whether $i$ and $j$ are connected from a single embedding $h_i$, which only ``sees''
about $3^L$ hops; the similarity read-out compares the two embeddings, so the pair
is resolved once their two $3^L$-hop neighbourhoods overlap (roughly $2\cdot 3^L$).
What the read-out cannot do is cross a bottleneck: the barbell stays at exact
$0.00$ for both read-outs and all four seeds (Section~\ref{sec:diff}), and on the
connected pairs the similarity read-out is even slightly worse there (reach
$\approx0.30$ vs.\ $\approx0.48$ for linear). Comparing two embeddings does not help
when the information cannot cross the cut in the first place; that limit belongs to
the trunk, and would need more depth, not a different read-out.

\paragraph{The read-out doubles the reach radius; it does not change how the wall
scales with depth.} A natural question is whether the similarity read-out lifts the
distance limit by a fixed amount or removes the dependence on depth altogether. To
check, we repeat the depth sweep of Report~III (train at $n=64$ on the path-union
distribution, vary only the number of layers $L$) with the similarity read-out and
compare to the linear sweep. Table~\ref{tab:depthsim} shows the linear read-out
resolves pairs up to $3^L$ ($d=3$ for $L=1$, $d=9$ for $L=2$); the similarity
read-out, on the same model, resolves up to twice that ($d=6$ for $L=1$, $d=18$ for
$L=2$) and then degrades, with the same recovery on the farthest pairs. So the
read-out buys a factor of $2$, but the wall still triples per layer --- it
multiplies the capacity, it does not remove the distance dependence. (At $L=3$ both
read-outs are perfect across the whole $n=64$ range, so this $n$ is too small to
tell them apart; a larger $n$ would be needed.) Reassuringly, the $d=18$ point
measured here at $n=64$ is the same place the $n=40$ chain\_plus curve
(Table~\ref{tab:chainplus_n40}) bends, so two different experiments agree on the
constant. The effect also shows in-distribution: at $L=2$ the similarity read-out
lifts exact match on the held-out path-union test from $0.40$ to $0.62$.

\subsection{Two ways to be wrong: under-reaching and over-connecting}
\label{sec:reachcut}

So far we have talked about \emph{reach}: getting the connected pairs right. The
model can be wrong in a second way too. It can \emph{under-reach} --- miss a
far-but-connected pair, which is the $3^L$ wall we have been studying --- or it can
\emph{over-connect} --- wrongly join two nodes that are in different components (a
``cut'' failure). These two are not the same thing. A model that computed
reachability cleanly would have the reach wall but would still cut perfectly, because
two separate components share no path at any length, short or long. (We checked this
with a simple matrix-power oracle: it cuts every disconnected pair correctly, no
matter how the graph is arranged.) So a cut failure is not the $3^L$ wall --- it is
a sign that the model's embeddings are imperfect and leak some ``connectedness''
across a real gap.

What we find is that the standard model does both kinds of error, and on the same
families. Table~\ref{tab:reachcut} reports, on the $n=40$ mixed runs, the reach
accuracy (connected pairs) and the cut accuracy (disconnected pairs) for the two
read-outs. The linear read-out loses cut accuracy exactly on the long families ---
chain\_plus ($0.89$) and path\_union ($0.97$) --- the same ones where it also
under-reaches. The interesting part is how the two interventions behave. The
similarity read-out improves reach \emph{and} cut on those families (chain\_plus cut
$0.89\to0.99$), with no trade-off, because $\cos(h_i,h_j)$ keeps genuinely separate
components apart (their embeddings point in different directions) while still joining
far-but-connected pairs. The Laplacian loss does the opposite: it buys reach by
\emph{spending} cut (on ER-trained 2chains it keeps reach at $1.00$ but lowers cut
from $0.99$ to $0.94$), because pulling all connected embeddings together also blurs
the boundary between components. So if the goal is a better model, the read-out is
the better lever --- it helps both errors at once; the loss only moves the problem
from one to the other.

What the table does not tell us is \emph{what kind} of error the cut failure is ---
in particular, whether the linear model over-connects two components more when they
were ``almost'' joined. We test that directly with a small controlled experiment
(the near-miss cut, Section~\ref{sec:nearmiss}).

\section{Thread 2: the diameter, or also the bottleneck?}
\label{sec:diff}

\subsection{The difficulty map}
\label{sec:diffmap}

\paragraph{Purpose.} This is the question the project was originally set to answer:
\emph{how does the standard model's accuracy vary with the graph's diameter, and
with its spectral gap, and which of the two is the operative variable?} The
family-by-family numbers below (Table~\ref{tab:fam}) hint at an answer but are
unsatisfying, because each family is a single, isolated point in the (diameter,
gap) plane --- a chain is one corner, an expander another --- so one cannot read
off a clean dependence, let alone separate the two axes. To do that properly we
need (i) graphs that \emph{spread continuously} across the plane and (ii) the
\emph{joint} per-graph record of diameter, gap and accuracy together, which the
per-family evaluation discards.

\paragraph{Method.} We therefore evaluate the standard (linear read-out) model on
a purpose-built pool of single-component graphs that spans the plane: an
Erd\H os--R\'enyi density sweep (the main arc, dense/small-diameter/large-gap down
to sparse/large-diameter/small-gap), a random-regular degree sweep (which moves
the gap at a roughly fixed diameter band --- degree $2$ is a cycle, high degree is
near-complete), a barbell clique-size sweep (the small-gap bottleneck corner), and
single chains and cycles (the large-diameter corner). For \emph{every} graph we
log its diameter, its spectral gap, its mean degree (a density control), and the
model's exact-match and pairwise accuracy. From this we build the two things
the question actually needs: a \textbf{two-dimensional accuracy map} over
(diameter, gap), and the \textbf{partial-dependence} curves --- accuracy versus
diameter at a fixed gap, and accuracy versus gap at a fixed diameter --- which are
what disentangle the two variables (and, by conditioning on mean degree, separate
both from a mere density effect). The evaluation is run on the standard linear
checkpoints at $n=20$ (eight reproduction seeds) and $n=40$ (the ER-trained
family seeds), so the map is reported with a seed band, not a single draw.

\paragraph{What the map shows: two axes we can tell apart (capacity and density),
and a gap we cannot.} The map (Figure~\ref{fig:diffmap}) and the partial-dependence
curves (Figure~\ref{fig:diffmap-pd}) give a clearer answer than the family panel,
and it is partly different from what we expected. Two axes come out clearly
separable.
\emph{(1) Diameter / capacity.} Once we control for density (restrict to graphs
near the training density, mean degree $\le 4$), accuracy is near-perfect within
capacity ($d\le 9$) and drops, non-monotonically, beyond it --- the $3^L$ wall of
Report~III, now visible on a continuous pool rather than at isolated family points.
\emph{(2) Density relative to training.} Independently of diameter, accuracy
collapses on graphs \emph{denser} than the ER training distribution
(Figure~\ref{fig:diffmap-pd}b): the $n=20$ model falls off above mean degree
$\approx6$, and the $n=40$ model --- trained on the sparser $\mathrm{ER}(40,0.05)$
--- tolerates almost no excess density, reaching near-zero exact match already by
mean degree $4$. This density-out-of-distribution axis is a clean, separable
difficulty variable that the family panel under-weighted, and it dominates the
``large-gap is hard'' impression of Table~\ref{tab:fam}: large gap means dense
means out-of-distribution, not gap-the-quantity.

\emph{(3) The spectral gap does not come out as an independent axis} --- and not
for lack of an effect, but because it is \emph{not identifiable} in any natural
pool. Among sparse connected graphs the diameter and the (log) spectral gap are
collinear (correlation $-0.92$): a long sparse graph necessarily has both a large
diameter \emph{and} a small gap, so the plane has no region with large diameter and
large gap (or the reverse) from which to read a partial dependence --- the
off-diagonal cells of the diameter--gap map are simply empty, and the
``accuracy vs gap'' curves separate cleanly \emph{by diameter band}
(Figure~\ref{fig:diffmap-pd}c), i.e.\ the gap is tracking the diameter. The one
structure that breaks the collinearity --- the barbell, a moderate diameter with a
tiny gap --- does so only by introducing local density (its two cliques push the
mean degree to $4$--$9$), so it moves onto the density axis instead. You cannot, with
natural graphs, vary the gap at fixed diameter \emph{and} fixed density: a
bottleneck is a dense-blobs-plus-thin-bridge object, gap-entangled-with-density by
construction. This sharpens rather than refutes the earlier reading: the $3^L$
diameter wall is real and separable, a bottleneck genuinely adds difficulty
(Section~\ref{sec:diff}), but the scalar spectral gap is the \emph{wrong}
independent variable to express it --- collinear with diameter on sparse graphs and
with density on bottlenecked ones --- which is exactly why every gap probe in this
report carries a residual confound. The honest map-level statement is therefore:
difficulty is governed by \emph{distance-versus-capacity} and by
\emph{density-versus-training}, with the bottleneck a real but non-separable third
effect.

is the \emph{barbell\_var} sweep reported there. The reading stays ``diameter

yet pinned to a single isolated number.

\section{Thread 3: the spectral gap, and a confound}
\label{sec:gap}

\paragraph{A first, failed attempt.} To vary the gap continuously we evaluate
\emph{expander\_var} (a random regular graph of varying degree, hence varying gap),
single-component, so it is pure reach. The accuracy by gap (baseline ER/linear,
$n=20$, mean over the eight seeds) is \emph{not} monotone:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
gap & $0.04$--$0.08$ & $0.08$--$0.15$ & $0.15$--$0.30$ & $0.30$--$0.60$ & $0.60$--$1.0$ \\
\midrule
exact & 0.59 & 0.98 & 0.99 & 0.80 & 0.51 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The reason is a confound we did not anticipate: increasing the degree raises the
gap \emph{but also the density}, and a high-degree graph has far more edges than
the sparse $\mathrm{ER}(20,0.08)$ the model was trained on --- it is
\emph{out-of-distribution in density}, not hard because of the gap. So
expander\_var does not isolate the gap (it varies two things at once); the
rising part (gap $0.04\!\to\!0.15$) is genuine, the falling part is the confound.

\paragraph{The intended clean test: distance fixed, paths varied.} To isolate the
gap from both distance and density we designed \emph{parallel paths}: two
terminals joined by $k$ disjoint paths of length $\ell$. The distance is $\ell$
for \emph{every} $k$, but the effective resistance (how hard it is to push
information across, treating each edge as a unit resistor) is $\ell/k$, falling
with $k$. Varying $k$ at fixed $\ell$ should separate the two hypotheses: if
difficulty is \emph{distance} (the paper) the terminal error is flat in $k$; if it
is \emph{resistance / bottleneck} (our reading) it falls with $k$. We ran this on
the $n=64$ reach checkpoints ($L=1,2,3$), recording both the terminal accuracy and
the model's true end-to-end influence $\|\partial h_t/\partial x_s\|$.

\paragraph{The probe is inconclusive: a distribution-shift confound.} The result
does not separate the hypotheses, because the construction introduces a confound
of its own. The terminal accuracy is \emph{non-monotone} in $k$ (it often
\emph{collapses} for $k\ge3$, the opposite of the resistance prediction), and the
influence spikes erratically. The tell is the $k=1$ column --- a single path, the
purest in-distribution case --- in Table~\ref{tab:parpaths}: at $L=2$ a single
path of length $\ell=5$, a distance \emph{well within} the capacity $9$, scores
only $0.15$, when the same $L=2$ model resolves $d=5$ perfectly on every
in-distribution family. The reason is that a parallel-paths graph is a handful of
active nodes ($2+k(\ell-1)$) floating in a $64$-node canvas of isolated padding,
with terminals of degree $k$ --- structurally unlike the path-union training
(which fills all $64$ nodes with degree-$\le2$ paths). Raising $k$ makes the
terminals higher-degree hubs, pushing the graph further out of distribution. So
parallel paths varies resistance \emph{and} distribution at once, exactly the kind
of confound we flagged for expander\_var; it does not isolate the gap. A clean
redesign (fill the canvas, hold the terminal degree fixed, or measure resistance
on in-distribution graphs) is needed.

\paragraph{What \emph{does} survive: a graded bottleneck (barbell\_var).} The gap
signal is nonetheless real, and visible in a construction that stays inside one
topology. \emph{barbell\_var} varies the strength of the bottleneck (bridge and
clique sizes) while keeping the barbell shape, so increasing the gap loosens the
cut. Sorted by gap (Table~\ref{tab:barbellvar}), the difficulty falls
monotonically with the gap, and --- importantly --- the linear and similarity
read-outs behave \emph{identically}, confirming that the read-out (which moves the
distance wall) does nothing for a bottleneck. This is the graded, in-topology
version of the within-capacity barbell collapse of Section~\ref{sec:diff}.

\paragraph{Where this leaves the difficulty question.} Three different
constructions now point the same way: the barbell fails inside the capacity
(Section~\ref{sec:diff}), barbell\_var gets monotonically easier as the gap
widens, and expander\_var fails only at the smallest gaps. A small gap (a
bottleneck) makes a pair hard over and above its distance. But no single probe
isolates the gap from everything else --- expander\_var co-varies it with density,
barbell\_var with bridge geometry, and parallel paths with distribution --- so we
report a robust \emph{direction} (gap matters, monotonically) without yet a clean
scalar law. The honest statement remains ``diameter \emph{and} a bottleneck
term''.

\section{A direct test: the near-miss cut}
\label{sec:nearmiss}

Section~\ref{sec:reachcut} showed the linear model over-connects on the long
families. Here we ask what that error depends on. We take a single chain on all $n$
nodes, remove one edge so it splits into two components, and look at the
disconnected (cross-component) pairs. For each such pair we record how far apart the
two nodes \emph{would} be if the edge were put back --- their distance along the
original chain --- and call it the \emph{near-miss distance}. We then plot the cut
accuracy (fraction of these pairs correctly called disconnected) against the
near-miss distance. On the same graphs we also plot the ordinary reach (the
connected pairs, by distance), so the two curves share one x-axis. This is eval-only
on the checkpoints we already have.

There is a clear baseline to compare against. A clean matrix-power model cuts
\emph{every} disconnected pair correctly, at every near-miss distance, because once
the edge is removed there is genuinely no path between the two sides (we verified
this with an oracle). So a flat cut accuracy at $1.0$ would mean the model behaves
like clean matrix powering here; a \emph{dip at small near-miss distances} would mean
the opposite --- that the model's embedding still remembers the almost-path and
over-connects the two sides when they were close. The experiment tells us which of
the two the standard model is, and whether the similarity read-out (which cleans up
the cut overall, Table~\ref{tab:reachcut}) also removes any such near-miss dip.

\section{A third failure mode: the degree heuristic}
\label{sec:third}

Separating connected (``reach'') from disconnected (``cut'') pairs is useful but
does not explain everything. On the two-cliques family the model often gets even
the distance-$1$ pairs wrong --- pairs that are directly adjacent inside a clique.
At $n=20$ this within-clique accuracy averages $0.71$ over the eight seeds; at
$n=40$, ER-trained, it is lower and very seed-dependent ($0.00$ to $0.96$ across the
four seeds, $0.30$ at seed $1000$). The model mis-classifies \emph{directly
connected} pairs in a dense graph: the signature of the \textbf{degree heuristic}
(every node in a clique has high degree, and the model is misled), not a reach or a
cut limitation. There are thus three ways the model fails: it cannot reach across a
bottleneck, it sometimes cannot cut, and on dense graphs it falls back on degree.

Unlike the bottleneck, this third mode is \emph{recoverable}, but the four-seed
data sharpens \emph{how}. At $n=40$ the ER-trained linear model gets only
$\approx0.3$ on the $d=1$ clique pairs of 2cliques --- and very erratically so
across seeds ($0.30, 0.00, 0.96, 0.03$) --- with exact match $0$. Two interventions
help, at two different levels. \emph{Either} the similarity read-out \emph{or}
mixed training lifts the within-clique reach (the $d=1$ pairs) to $1.00$, so the
degree-heuristic error itself is removed by either lever. But the \emph{whole-graph}
2cliques solution (exact match $1.00$) is recovered \emph{only by mixed training}:
the ER-trained similarity model has reach $1.00$ yet exact match still $0$, because
it fixes the within-clique connections but not the across-clique \emph{cut} (the
two cliques are separate components, and ER-only training never shows the model a
dense block it must split off). So similarity repairs the degree heuristic at the
pair level; solving 2cliques end-to-end needs the dense disconnected examples that
only mixed training supplies. The degree heuristic is therefore a property of the
narrow ER-only, linear-read-out baseline, not an intrinsic limit of the
architecture --- in contrast to the barbell bottleneck, which survives both
interventions.

\section{Takeaway}

The family-level numbers in this report are now averaged over several seeds (four at
$n=40$, eight at $n=20$), so the conclusions below are not based on a single lucky
run; only the $n=64$ depth sweep and the parallel-paths probe remain single-seed.
The two questions stand as follows.

\emph{On the architecture (Thread~1).} The Laplacian loss is redundant under mixed
training (the read-out already drives the smoothness term to zero), but it is not a
no-op on the narrower ER baseline, where it acts as a connection-vs-cut lever ---
helping long-range reach (chain\_plus exact $0.11\to0.41$) at the cost of the cut
(2chains $0.47\to0.20$); we drop it for the main results. The similarity read-out
helps more than we first thought, and we can put a number on it: it doubles the
reach radius, from $3^L$ to $2\cdot 3^L$ (measured at $L=1$ and $L=2$ on the same
model, Table~\ref{tab:depthsim}, and reproduced on the held-out chain\_plus at
$n=40$ across four seeds). The wall still triples per layer, so this is a fixed
factor of $2$, not a change of behaviour: the read-out compares two
$3^L$-hop neighbourhoods that meet in the middle, instead of looking at one. The one
thing the read-out cannot fix is the bottleneck --- the barbell stays at exact $0$
for both read-outs and all four seeds (and is even slightly worse on the connected
pairs) --- which is a property of the trunk and would need more depth. A separate,
milder lever is the training data rather than the read-out: mixed training is what
removes the degree-heuristic failure on dense graphs end-to-end
(Section~\ref{sec:third}).

\emph{On difficulty (Threads~2--3).} The difficulty map gives the cleanest answer:
two axes we can tell apart. One is distance-versus-capacity (the $3^L$ wall, now
visible on a continuous, density-controlled pool). The other is
density-versus-training (graphs denser than the ER training set are
out-of-distribution and fail --- the $n=40$ model almost immediately). The spectral
gap is \emph{not} a third axis we can separate: on sparse graphs it is essentially a
stand-in for the diameter (correlation $-0.92$), and on the one structure that
breaks that link --- the barbell --- it comes tied to density. A bottleneck does
make graphs harder: at $n=40$ the barbell already fails at $d=3$, four times inside
the capacity, where every non-bottleneck family is solved. But we cannot reduce that
to the gap as a single number, which is why each attempt to isolate it kept a
left-over confound (parallel-paths a distribution shift, barbell\_var the bridge
geometry, expander\_var the density). So we can say a smaller gap is harder, as a
direction, but not write it as a clean law. The degree-heuristic failure, in
contrast, is recoverable: either the similarity read-out or mixed training fixes the
within-clique pairs, though only mixed training solves the whole 2cliques graph.

\paragraph{Limitations and next steps.} The similarity read-out and the Laplacian
loss are non-standard choices, so they are best read as diagnostics (``what is the
trunk missing'') rather than as a recommended architecture. The depth sweep cannot
separate the two read-outs at $L=3$ because $n=64$ already saturates; a larger $n$
would be needed. And we never found a construction that varies the spectral gap
while holding both the diameter and the density fixed --- which, the map suggests,
may not be possible with natural graphs --- so the bottleneck effect stays a
direction, not an isolated number.
