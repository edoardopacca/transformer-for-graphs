\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This is the third report in the series. The earlier reports studied a
\emph{standard} (non-disentangled) transformer trained to predict the
connectivity matrix of an input graph, and asked whether the \emph{data lever}
of Ye, Fu, Jia and Sharan (2026) --- restricting the training data to
within-capacity graphs (diameter $\le 3^L$) --- transfers from the disentangled
transformer (for which it is proven) to a standard transformer (for which the
paper only shows empirical evidence). This report is organised in two parts.

\paragraph{Part~I --- the data lever.} We reproduce the paper's own
standard-transformer study at $n = 20$ under its exact configuration and ask
whether training on diameter-restricted data really improves out-of-distribution
generalisation, as its Figures~7/11 suggest. Running many seeds, we find that it
does not: whether a model recovers the generalising (algorithmic) solution is
governed by the random seed, not by the diameter filter. The clean separation
the paper presents as a phase transition is, in our hands, a high-variance event,
and the diameter-restricted and unrestricted conditions generalise at
statistically indistinguishable rates. So the claim that ``more within-capacity
data'' helps a standard transformer is not supported by our experiments.

\paragraph{Part~II --- the capacity wall.} Across every structured test we ran
(2-chains, single and double cycles), the $n = 40$ models fail on node pairs at
shortest-path distance beyond roughly $9$, while being near-perfect below it.
We characterise this wall: we show it is the $3^L$ matrix-powering capacity of an
$L$-layer model (with $L = 2$, $3^2 = 9$), localise the error, show that the same
trunk can nonetheless \emph{count} connected components trivially, and finally
vary the depth $L$ directly to confirm that the wall moves with depth.

Throughout, when we report $n = 40$ results we also report a second independent
training round (``exp2'') to expose run-to-run variance, and we state the
training distribution and the test distributions at the start of each section.

\section{Model and task}
\label{sec:model}

The task is unchanged from the earlier reports. Given an undirected graph on $n$
nodes, the input is its self-loop-augmented adjacency matrix
$A \in \{0,1\}^{n \times n}$ and the target is the connectivity matrix
$R \in \{0,1\}^{n \times n}$ with $R_{ij} = 1$ iff $i$ and $j$ lie in the same
connected component. We report \textbf{exact-match accuracy} (fraction of test
graphs predicted perfectly) and \textbf{pairwise accuracy} (fraction of
correctly predicted node pairs), optionally broken down by shortest-path
distance $d$.

Three model families appear in this report.

\paragraph{Large $n = 40$ connectivity models} (Part~II,
Sections~\ref{sec:cycles}, \ref{sec:pairwise}). $L = 2$ layers, $4$ attention
heads, $d_{\mathrm{model}} = 512$, $d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 2048$, normalised-ReLU
attention ($\alpha = \tfrac{1}{n}\mathrm{ReLU}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_h})$), pre-norm
LayerNorm, GELU feed-forward, no causal mask and no positional encoding;
$\sim 6.3$M parameters. Trained on \textbf{Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi
$\mathrm{ER}(n = 40, p = 0.05)$} streamed online (each graph seen once,
$\approx 10^9$ graphs), batch $1{,}000$, $1{,}000{,}000$ steps, AdamW, peak lr
$10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, cosine schedule, bf16. The four runs differ
only in the diameter filter on the training stream: \emph{unfiltered}, $D \le
11$, $D \le 9$ ($= 3^L$), $D \le 7$.

\paragraph{Component classifier} (Section~\ref{sec:count}). Same trunk, with the
$n \times n$ read-out replaced by a mean-pool over node embeddings and a
single-logit head; binary cross-entropy.

\paragraph{Depth-sweep reach models} (Section~\ref{sec:reach}). The same big
trunk but with the number of layers $L$ varied in $\{1,2,3,4\}$, trained at
$n = 64$ on a purpose-built ``path-union'' distribution (defined there) to
measure how far each depth can compute reachability.

The reproduction of Part~I uses a separate $n = 20$ configuration described in
Section~\ref{sec:repro}.

\section{Reproducing the paper's standard-transformer experiment ($n = 20$)}
\label{sec:repro}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Goal.} Reproduce the paper's preliminary standard-transformer study
--- its Figure~1 (failure to generalise) and its data-lever Figures~7 and~11 ---
under the paper's exact configuration, and ask whether the data lever produces
the OOD benefit it claims at $n = 20$.

\paragraph{Architecture.} As specified in the paper (\S3.3 and Appendix~A.1/D.1):
a $2$-layer standard transformer, \textbf{single-head} self-attention,
$d_{\mathrm{model}} = 512$, $d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 2048$, normalised-ReLU attention
$\alpha = \tfrac{1}{n}\mathrm{ReLU}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_h})$, GELU feed-forward, no
positional encoding. AdamW, peak lr $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, cosine
schedule, batch size $1{,}000$, $1{,}000{,}000$ steps, each graph seen once
($\approx 10^9$ graphs), bf16.

Crucially, the paper does not pin down the \emph{exact} architecture it trains.
Appendix~A.1 gives an idealised mathematical specification (pre-LayerNorm,
bias-free read-in/read-out, no dropout), whereas Appendix~D.1 says the trained
standard transformers \emph{``adopt the implementation from RoBERTa''} --- which
in practice implies post-LayerNorm blocks, dropout $0.1$ and a
$\mathcal{N}(0, 0.02)$ initialisation --- without stating those choices
explicitly. The two readings differ exactly in the details that shape the
training dynamics, which is what the paper's claim is about. Rather than guess,
we ran \textbf{both}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item an \textbf{A.1-style} model (pre-LayerNorm, no dropout, default
          initialisation), and
    \item a \textbf{RoBERTa-faithful} model (post-LayerNorm, dropout $0.1$,
          $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.02)$ initialisation).
\end{itemize}
We treat these as two settings of one experiment, not as two experiments.

\paragraph{Training and test distributions.} Training on
\textbf{$\mathrm{ER}(n = 20, p = 0.08)$}, in two conditions: \emph{unrestricted}
and \emph{restricted} to diameter $\le 9$ ($= 3^L$, the data lever). Test on the
two out-of-distribution families used by the paper, both at $n = 20$:
\textbf{2Chain$(n = 20, k = 10)$} (two disjoint paths of $10$ nodes) and
\textbf{2Clique$(n = 20, k = 10)$} (two disjoint $10$-cliques). The metric is
exact-match accuracy, exactly as in the paper (\S3.3: the product over all node
pairs).

\paragraph{What ``seed'' controls.} In our runs a single integer seed sets, at
once, (i) the \textbf{weight initialisation} and (ii) the \textbf{online
training-graph stream} (which graphs are sampled, and in which order). The two
out-of-distribution test sets (2Chain, 2Clique) are generated from \emph{fixed}
seeds and are therefore identical across all runs. Hence any run-to-run
difference in OOD generalisation reported below comes from initialisation and
training stochasticity, not from a different test set; our setup couples those
two sources and cannot separate them (a clean follow-up would fix one and vary
the other).

\paragraph{One favourable seed looks exactly like the paper.} Restricted
seed~$6000$ lifts \emph{both} 2Chain ($0.99$) and 2Clique ($0.81$): shown on its

as Figures~7/11. But it is $1$ of $8$. Reading down Table~\ref{tab:repro-rob}, in
each condition only about $3$ of $8$ seeds generalise on any OOD family and the
rest sit at zero; the diameter filter does not help on balance (restricted is
slightly better on 2Chain --- $3/8$ vs.\ $2/8$ --- and \emph{worse} on 2Clique ---
$1/8$ vs.\ $3/8$). Figure~\ref{fig:seed-examples} makes the accident explicit by
putting the restricted and unrestricted curves \emph{side by side} for three
seeds: for seed~$6000$ restriction clearly helps (the paper's story), but for
seed~$7000$ it is the \emph{opposite} --- the unrestricted model generalises and
the restricted one fails outright --- and for seed~$1000$ it is mixed (restricted
wins on 2Chain, unrestricted wins on 2Clique). The diameter filter is not a
consistent improvement; it helps, hurts, or does nothing depending on the seed.

\paragraph{The seed, not the filter, is the dominant axis of variation.} The
RoBERTa details \emph{do} unlock the algorithmic solution the A.1 model never
reached (a run can hit 2Clique $= 1.000$ unrestricted, 2Chain $= 1.000$
restricted), but \emph{whether} a run finds it is a coin-flip: $\sim 3/8$ of seeds
generalise in each condition. The winning seeds even specialise differently ---
some solve 2Clique (all pairs at distance $1$) but lag on 2Chain (hardest pairs
sit at the capacity boundary $d = 9$) and vice-versa --- consistent with the
$3^L$ capacity picture of Part~II.

\paragraph{Conclusion of Part~I.} At $n = 20$ the restricted and unrestricted
conditions generalise at essentially the same (low) rate
(Table~\ref{tab:repro-rob}): the restricted model is not better overall and is
worse on 2Clique. We therefore do not find support for the paper's claim that
restricting the training data to within-capacity graphs improves OOD
generalisation of a \emph{standard} transformer. We robustly reproduce its
Figure~1 (failure), and confirm the generalising solution is \emph{reachable};
but its appearance is governed by initialisation, and a single favourable seed
reproduces the appearance of a clean Figure~7/11 phase transition. This is
consistent with our earlier reports: with the architectures of Reports~1 and~2
(the softmax and normalised-ReLU $n = 40$ models, evaluated at length there), the
data lever likewise did not improve OOD generalisation --- the unfiltered model
was at least as good as every diameter-restricted specialist. Establishing a
genuine data-lever effect would require many more seeds; with the variance we
observe, the diameter filter is not the deciding factor.

\section{Cycle graphs: connection vs.\ disconnection}
\label{sec:cycles}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Models.} The four large $n = 40$ connectivity models of
Section~\ref{sec:model}, trained on $\mathrm{ER}(n = 40, p = 0.05)$
(unfiltered, $D \le 11$, $D \le 9$, $D \le 7$).

\paragraph{Test distributions.} Two structured families, both on the full
$n = 40$ nodes (no isolated padding; $n_{\text{active}} = 40$), each a test set
of $10{,}000$ graphs with random node permutations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1cycle:} a single cycle $C_{40}$ through all $40$ nodes ---
          one connected component, every node of degree $2$, diameter $20$.
          The target $R$ is the all-ones matrix.
    \item \textbf{2cycle:} two disjoint cycles $2 \times C_{20}$ --- two
          connected components of $20$ nodes each, every node of degree $2$,
          component diameter $10$. The target $R$ is block-diagonal.
\end{itemize}
These two distributions are extreme opposites: on 1cycle a model must confirm
that \emph{every} pair is connected (including pairs at distance up to $20$),
whereas on 2cycle it must additionally \emph{cut} the graph into the two
correct components.

\paragraph{What the table and figure show.} There is a clean cross-over.
The $D \le 11$ model is best on 1cycle (pairwise $1.000$, exact $0.943$) but
collapses to chance ($0.500$) on 2cycle: it behaves like ``predict connected
everywhere'', which is exactly right when the graph is a single component and
exactly $50\%$ wrong when it is two. The tightly filtered models $D \le 9$ and
$D \le 7$ are the mirror image: they are best at \emph{separating} the two
cycles ($0.975$ and $0.860$ on 2cycle) but poor on 1cycle ($0.475$, $0.360$),
because a single $C_{40}$ requires confirming connections up to distance $20$,
far beyond their effective range. The unfiltered model sits in between
($\approx 0.73$ on both). In short, a tighter diameter budget trades the ability
to confirm long-range connection for the ability to detect disconnection.

\section{Where the 2-chain error lives}
\label{sec:pairwise}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Models.} The same four large $n = 40$ models trained on
$\mathrm{ER}(n = 40, p = 0.05)$.

\paragraph{Test distribution.} \textbf{2-chains} at the full size
$n = 40$, $k = n/2 = 20$: two disjoint paths of $20$ nodes each (two connected
components), random node permutations, $5{,}000$ graphs. A $20$-node path has
diameter $19$, so it contains within-component pairs at distances well beyond the
capacity $3^L = 9$.

On this distribution the models reach $\sim 80\%$ pairwise accuracy but $0\%$
exact match, and the exact-match accuracy collapses from $1.0$ to $0.0$ exactly
at $n_{\text{active}} = 22$. We decompose the pairwise error: for each model we
split the off-diagonal pairs into within-component pairs at $d \le 9$ (within
capacity), within-component pairs at $d > 9$ (beyond capacity), and
across-component pairs (target $0$).

\paragraph{What the table and figures show.} The $\sim 80\%$ pairwise accuracy is
not a heuristic artefact: the error is concentrated almost entirely in the
within-component pairs \emph{beyond} capacity ($d > 9$). The capacity-respecting
models $D \le 9$ and $D \le 7$ get every across-component pair right
(disconnected accuracy $1.000$, zero false positives) and every within-capacity
pair right, but score $0.000$ on $d > 9$ pairs --- they implement ``connected
$\iff d \le 9$''. Because every 2-chain with $k = 20$ contains pairs at
distances $10$--$19$, every graph has wrong pairs, so exact match is $0$ while
pairwise stays high. This also explains the cliff at $n_{\text{active}} = 22$:
$n_{\text{active}} = 20$ means chains of length $10$ (maximum distance $9 =$
capacity), the last fully solvable size; $n_{\text{active}} = 22$ introduces
distance $10 > 9$ and breaks exact match.

\section{Counting components: 1-chain vs.\ 2-chain}
\label{sec:count}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Task.} A \emph{graph-level binary} task: decide whether the input
graph is a single chain (one connected component, label $0$) or two chains (two
connected components, label $1$). This is the ``$+1$ for 2-chains, $-1$ for
1-chain'' classification, mapped to BCE targets $\{1, 0\}$.

\paragraph{Architecture.} The component classifier of Section~\ref{sec:model}:
the same large $n = 40$ trunk ($d_{\mathrm{model}} = 512$, $4$ heads,
$d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 2048$, normalised-ReLU attention, $2$ layers) with a
mean-pool + single-logit head. AdamW, peak lr $10^{-4}$, weight decay
$10^{-4}$, cosine schedule, batch size $1{,}000$, $200{,}000$ steps, bf16.

\paragraph{Training and test distributions.} Online stream at $n = 40$, $50/50$
between a \textbf{1-chain} (a single path over all $40$ nodes) and a
\textbf{2-chain} ($k = 20$: two disjoint paths of $20$ nodes), node order
randomly permuted. The two classes are nearly indistinguishable by cheap global
cues: both use all $40$ nodes, the edge counts are $39$ vs.\ $38$, and the
degree histograms differ only by $2$ vs.\ $4$ degree-$1$ endpoints. The test set
is balanced ($5{,}000$ of each class). We ran \textbf{2 iterations}
(seeds $1000$, $2000$).

\section{Depth controls reach: is the wall at $3^L$?}
\label{sec:reach}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Question.} Every section above shows the wall at distance $\approx 9$
for our $L = 2$ models, and $3^2 = 9$ is the matrix-powering capacity an
$L$-layer model is predicted to reach. We test this \emph{causally} by varying
only the depth $L$ and measuring the maximum distance the model can resolve.

\paragraph{Training distribution (``path-union'', $n = 64$).} To measure reach we
need training graphs that actually contain long shortest paths --- ER at $n = 40$
tops out around distance $20$, too short to probe $3^3 = 27$. We therefore train
at $n = 64$ on \textbf{disjoint unions of random paths}, built as follows: for
each graph we draw the number of paths $k$ \emph{uniformly at random from}
$\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$, partition the $64$ nodes into $k$ contiguous segments (random
split), turn each segment into a path, and finally permute the node labels.
Hence $\approx 25\%$ of graphs are a single $64$-node path (shortest-path
distances up to $63$), $\approx 25\%$ split into two paths, and so on; whenever
$k > 1$ there are genuine cross-path disconnections, so the model cannot trivially
predict ``all connected''. The connectivity target is block-diagonal by path.
There is \emph{no} diameter restriction --- the point here is the opposite, to
expose long distances.

\paragraph{Model (exactly which one).} We use the \emph{same architecture as the
$n = 40$ connectivity models of Section~\ref{sec:model}}: the pre-norm
\texttt{GraphConnectivityTransformer} with $4$ heads, $d_{\mathrm{model}} = 512$,
$d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 2048$, normalised-ReLU attention, GELU feed-forward and a
symmetric $n \times n$ read-out. This is the A.1-style model of Part~I, \emph{not}
the RoBERTa variant. We change \emph{only} the number of layers $L \in
\{1,2,3,4\}$ and train each to $1{,}000{,}000$ steps.

\paragraph{Metric.} Rather than collapse everything into a single ``reach
radius'', we report --- as for 2Chain/2Clique above --- (a) the
\textbf{in-distribution exact-match accuracy} on a held-out path-union test set,
and (b) the \textbf{pairwise accuracy on within-component pairs as a function of
their shortest-path distance $d$}, with the number of test pairs at each $d$.
This shows directly \emph{which} node pairs each depth can resolve and where it
breaks.

\subsection{Results}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering

\toprule

\midrule
1 & 3  & $\approx 0.00$ & up to $d = 3$ \\

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \figorbox{runs/reach_depth/reach_by_distance.png}{1.0}
    \caption{Pairwise accuracy on within-component pairs vs.\ shortest-path
    distance $d$, one panel per depth $L$ (the count of test pairs is printed
    above each bar; the red line marks $3^L$ when it falls within range). Accuracy
    is near-perfect up to $d \approx 3^L$ and then collapses for $L = 1, 2$;
    for $L = 3, 4$ it stays near-perfect across the whole $n = 64$ range.}
    \label{fig:reach-by-distance}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Which distances each depth resolves.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$L = 1$ resolves pairs up to $d = 3$, $L = 2$ up to $d = 9$
          --- a $\times 3$ per layer.} The reach triples with each added layer,
          exactly the $3^L$ matrix-powering prediction. This is the direct,
          causal explanation of the distance-$9$ wall seen throughout Part~II:
          all our $n = 40$ models (and the paper's main ones) are $L = 2$, and
          $3^2 = 9$.
    \item \textbf{The wall is a depth limit.} At $L = 1, 2$ the model is
          near-perfect below the threshold and collapses above it, so almost no
          whole graph is exact (in-distribution exact match $\approx 0$ and
          $0.39$). A third layer removes the wall: $L = 3$ resolves every distance
          present and reaches exact match $1.00$.
    \item \textbf{$L = 2$ is capacity-saturated, not under-trained.} Its training
          loss plateaus around $0.14$ and its per-distance curve is flat-perfect
          to $d = 9$ then drops to zero: a $2$-layer model simply cannot fit the
          $d > 9$ pairs, and the non-zero loss floor is the signature of the wall
          (the $1$M-step re-run confirms the plateau).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Caveat (the high-$L$ behaviour).} The clean $\times 3$ reading is exact
at $L = 1, 2$; at $L = 3$ the model already resolves the full $n = 64$ range,
which is \emph{beyond} $3^3 = 27$. Two points. First, $n = 64$ caps the largest
measurable distance at $63$, so we cannot probe past it without a larger $n$.
Second, the paper's $3^L$ bound (Theorem~4.5) is \emph{worst-case existential} ---
it guarantees that \emph{some} diameter-$(3^L{+}1)$ graph defeats the model, not
that \emph{every} one does --- and path graphs are very low-degree, evidently not
that adversarial case. So this experiment cleanly fixes the $L = 2$ wall at
$3^2 = 9$ and shows it lifts with depth; locating the exact capacity of $L \ge 3$
would require adversarial graphs at $n > 64$ (a clean follow-up).
