\title{Empirical Analysis of Transformers on Graph Connectivity \\
       \large Part V: Is It Matrix Powering or DFS?}
\author{Edoardo Paccagnella}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent The earlier reports read the model's sharp distance-$3^L$ wall as evidence that
the standard transformer computes connectivity by \emph{matrix powering} ($A^{3^L}$). This
report asks a different question, suggested by the advisor: what if the model is instead
learning a \emph{depth-first search} (DFS) --- a sequential traversal that explores a
component node by node and runs out of budget after a bounded number of steps, rather than a
parallel computation bounded by shortest-path \emph{distance}? The two mechanisms make
opposite predictions on graphs that are \emph{short but large} --- in particular on two dense
cliques joined by a single bridge edge --- and on whether \emph{density} helps or hurts. This
report sets out the hypothesis, the predictions that separate it from matrix powering, and the
experiments we will run; results follow in later revisions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{What the first four reports established}
\label{sec:recap}

We start this report from a set of conclusions we now treat as established (each was
multi-seed and is documented in Reports~I--IV). They are the ground this report builds on, and
they are also the facts the DFS hypothesis must remain consistent with.

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.25em}
  \item \textbf{The data lever does not help the standard transformer generalise.} Restricting
        the training data to within-capacity graphs (diameter $\le 3^L$), which is \emph{proven}
        to help the disentangled transformer, does \emph{not} improve out-of-distribution
        generalisation of a \emph{standard} transformer. Reproducing the paper's $n=20$ setup
        over many seeds, the outcome is \textbf{seed-dominated}: whether a run finds the
        generalising solution is a coin-flip ($\sim 3/8$ seeds in either condition), and the
        clean ``phase transition'' the paper shows is, in our hands, a single lucky seed
        (Report~III, Part~I). The filter only speeds up convergence (Report~II).
  \item \textbf{There is a sharp wall at shortest-path distance $\approx 3^L$ ($=9$ for our
        $L=2$ models), and it is architectural, not a data gap.} The wall moves with depth in a
        causal depth sweep ($L=1\to d{=}3$, $L=2\to d{=}9$, $L=3\to$ the whole range;
        Report~III). It appears even on families that are \emph{in} the training stream and seen
        $\sim 10^8$ times (2chains, 1cycle at $n=40$: exactly $1.00$ up to $d=9$, then exactly
        $0.00$; Report~IV, Thread~1). So steering the data cannot move it.
  \item \textbf{Density is hard at inference --- but so far confounded with being
        out-of-distribution.} A graph denser than the (sparse) training set fails even when its
        diameter is tiny (Report~IV, Thread~1, density axis). We attributed this to the graph
        being unlike anything seen in training. The single sharpest instance is the
        \textbf{degree heuristic} on dense 2cliques, which is repaired \emph{only by the right
        data} (mixed training), not by the read-out or the loss (Report~IV, Thread~3).
  \item \textbf{The spectral gap is not a separable axis on natural graphs}
        (collinear with the diameter, $-0.82$); a real bottleneck does add difficulty, but only
        a purpose-built, confound-free probe isolates it (Report~IV, Thread~2).
  \item \textbf{A similarity read-out doubles the reach to $2\cdot 3^L$} (meet-in-the-middle of
        two $3^L$-hop neighbourhoods) and removes the over-connection (cut) error, \emph{but
        cannot cross a bottleneck} --- that limit belongs to the trunk (Report~IV, Thread~3).
  \item \textbf{Counting components is trivial} for the same trunk (1-chain vs 2-chain: $100\%$
        in $2{,}000$ steps), so the bottleneck of the connectivity task is not sensing global
        structure but producing correct long-range \emph{per-pair} reachability (Report~III).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The new question: matrix powering or DFS?}
\label{sec:question}

Everything above was \emph{interpreted} through one algorithm: matrix powering. An $L$-layer
model that repeatedly multiplies by the adjacency can confirm a connection within $3^L$ hops,
which fits the distance-$9$ wall exactly. But that wall is also consistent with a \emph{second}
algorithm we never separated from it: a \textbf{depth-first search}. A transformer cannot
recurse without bound in a fixed number of layers, so any DFS it learns must be a
\emph{bounded} traversal --- it explores a component step by step and \emph{stops after a
limited number of steps}, leaving the rest of the component unreached. The advisor's hypothesis
is that this, not matrix powering, may be what the model is doing: ``maybe the algorithm stops
after a certain iteration instead of running to the end, so it never finishes learning the
graph.''

The distinction matters because the two algorithms are bounded by \emph{different} quantities,
and on most graphs those quantities move together (a long graph has both a large distance and
many nodes to traverse), which is exactly why the first four reports could not tell them apart.
The point of this report is to build graphs where they \emph{disagree}.

\section{Two hypotheses, and how to tell them apart}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

\paragraph{Matrix powering (the reading of Reports~I--IV).} Connectivity via $A^{k}$ with
self-loops: $i$ and $j$ are connected iff $(A^{k})_{ij}>0$ for $k$ large enough. An $L$-layer
model realises this up to $k=3^L$. This computation is \emph{parallel} (every pair is resolved
at once), \emph{symmetric} (no preferred node or direction), and bounded by the
shortest-path \emph{distance} between the two nodes. Crucially, a \emph{denser} graph has
\emph{shorter} distances, so under matrix powering density should make connectivity
\emph{easier}, not harder.

\paragraph{DFS (the new hypothesis).} Connectivity by traversal: start at a node, walk into
neighbours, mark everything reachable as one component. A bounded DFS visits at most some fixed
number of nodes/edges before its budget runs out. This computation is \emph{sequential}, has a
\emph{starting point} (so it can resolve part of a component and not the rest), and is bounded
by the \emph{amount of structure to traverse} (number of nodes and edges), \emph{not} by
distance. Under DFS a \emph{denser} graph is \emph{harder}: each node has more neighbours, so a
fixed budget covers fewer nodes before it is exhausted.

\paragraph{Where they disagree.} Table~\ref{tab:predict} lists the predictions that separate
the two. The cleanest single discriminator is a graph that is \emph{short in distance but large
in nodes to traverse}: two dense cliques joined by a single edge. There the bridge sits at
distance $\le 3$ (trivial for matrix powering, well inside the $9$-hop capacity), but reaching
the far clique requires traversing one whole dense clique first (expensive for a bounded DFS).

A note on consistency with prior results. The DFS reading does \emph{not} overturn the $3^L$
wall: on the sparse, low-degree families of Reports~III--IV (chains, cycles, path unions) every
node has $\approx 2$ neighbours, so ``distance'' and ``nodes traversed along the path'' are the
same number, and a step-bounded DFS and a distance-bounded matrix power are
\emph{indistinguishable} --- both wall at $9$. That is precisely why those experiments could not
separate the two, and why the discriminating experiments below all break that coincidence
(dense local structure, or density swept on its own).

\section{The experiments we will run}
\label{sec:plan}

This section records the plan so the logic is legible to a future reader; results will be added
as the runs complete. All experiments use the standard RoBERTa-faithful transformer with the
linear read-out (the ``base'' model of Reports~III--IV), trained at $n=20$ and $n=40$, multi-seed,
unless noted.

\subsection{Core test: barbell (one bridge) vs.\ two cliques}
\label{sec:plan-barbell}

\paragraph{The two labels.} On $n$ nodes, split into a left half and a right half of $n/2$.
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.1em}
  \item \textbf{Label A --- bridged cliques (one component).} Each half is a clique; a
        \emph{single} edge joins one node of the left clique to one node of the right. The
        connectivity target $R$ is the all-ones matrix.
  \item \textbf{Label B --- two cliques (two components).} The same two cliques, with
        \emph{no} bridge. $R$ is block-diagonal (left block and right block all-ones,
        cross-block all-zeros).
\end{itemize}
The two graphs differ by exactly one edge, and that edge creates a connection at distance
$\le 3$. (This is \emph{not} the existing \texttt{generate\_barbell\_graph}, which joins
$\sim n/3$ cliques through a long middle path; we added two new generators,
\texttt{generate\_bridged\_cliques\_graph} and \texttt{generate\_split\_cliques\_graph}, both
taking a \texttt{clique\_size} so we can sweep it.) We run this two ways:

\emph{(a) Eval the base connectivity model.} The RoBERTa, linear read-out model of
Reports~III--IV, both ER-trained and mixed-trained, at $n=20$ and $n=40$, evaluated on the
connectivity matrix --- what the base model already does. (2cliques is in the mixed training
stream; the bridged version is held out, so it is the clean test.)

\emph{(b) A dedicated, trainable classifier.} A graph-level binary classifier
(\texttt{GraphBinaryClassifier}: the same standard trunk, mean-pool $+$ one logit) trained from
scratch on a 50/50 bridged/split stream, to ask whether the architecture can be \emph{taught}
to tell the two labels apart at all, and how that depends on the clique size. We train one model
with the clique size drawn at random per sample and read accuracy \emph{by} clique size (flat
$=$ matrix powering; falling at large $c$ $=$ a bounded-traversal limit), plus a few fixed-$c$
runs to time convergence against density.

\paragraph{What we measure.}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0.1em}
  \item \textbf{Can the base model tell A from B?} Both as the full connectivity matrix
        (does $\hat R$ contain the cross-block connection for A and not for B?) and, as a
        cleaner readout, as a graph-level discrimination of the two labels. Matrix powering
        $\Rightarrow$ easy at any clique size; DFS $\Rightarrow$ fails, and fails worse as the
        cliques grow denser/larger.
  \item \textbf{Where in $\hat R$ is the error (the ``stopped exploring'' signature)?} For
        label A, split the matrix into the left-clique block, the right-clique block, and the
        cross block, and report exact-match per block. The DFS signature is an \emph{asymmetry}:
        the clique containing the (implicit) start node is resolved (block $\approx 1.00$) while
        the cross block --- the far clique's connection to the near one --- is only partially
        right ($\approx 0.5$), because the traversal crossed the bridge but ran out of budget
        inside the far clique. Matrix powering predicts no such asymmetry.
  \item \textbf{Scaling with clique size.} Sweep $n/2 \in \{\dots\}$ (clique size). Under matrix
        powering the cross-block accuracy is flat in clique size (distance stays $\le 3$); under
        DFS it falls as the cliques grow (more to traverse before reaching / finishing the
        bridge).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Re-reading the existing barbell results for the same signature}
\label{sec:plan-reuse}

Report~IV already evaluated the (path-bridge) barbell per distance and found the mixed model
collapses at the bridge while getting within-clique pairs right. We will re-open those
predictions at the \emph{block} level (left clique / right clique / cross), to check for the
same asymmetry --- whether one side is resolved and the other is not --- without training
anything new. This is the cheap confirmation: the runs exist, we only add the per-block
breakdown.

\subsection{Density in optimisation, not just inference}
\label{sec:plan-density}

Reports~II--IV studied density (and diameter) at \emph{inference}. The DFS hypothesis makes a
prediction about \emph{training}: if the model learns a traversal whose cost grows with the
amount of structure, then \emph{denser} training graphs should make optimisation \emph{harder /
slower}, whereas matrix powering (shorter distances when dense) predicts the opposite. We will
sweep the training density (e.g.\ ER edge probability, or mean degree) at fixed $n$ and measure
convergence speed and final in-distribution accuracy, and the OOD transfer of each. We separate
this from the diameter sweep of Report~II (which varied distance, and found tighter $=$ faster):
here we hold the regime and vary \emph{density} on its own, the quantity a bounded traversal (DFS
or BFS) is sensitive to and matrix powering is not.

\subsection{Reference algorithms: matrix power, bounded DFS, and bounded BFS}
\label{sec:plan-oracle}

To make ``looks like a bounded traversal'' a measurement rather than a story, we implement
\emph{three} reference connectivity-matrix predictors (\texttt{dfs\_oracle.py}) and compare the
\emph{transformer's error pattern} to each. Crucially we keep the two visit-bounded traversals
\emph{separate}, because --- as the next paragraph shows --- they make \emph{opposite} predictions
on this graph, so they are not interchangeable and the experiment can tell them apart.
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.1em}
  \item a \textbf{matrix-power oracle}: $\mathbf{1}[(A+I)^{3^L}>0]$, the clean distance-bounded
        predictor (the cut oracle of Report~IV). On the bridged cliques it connects the two
        sides at \emph{any} clique size (the bridge is $\le 3$ hops), so its cross-block curve
        is flat at $1$.
  \item a \textbf{bounded-DFS oracle}: from each start node run a \emph{depth}-first search and
        stop after visiting a fixed \texttt{budget} of nodes, calling them connected.
  \item a \textbf{bounded-BFS oracle}: from each start node run a \emph{breadth}-first search
        (a ball of the \texttt{budget} nearest nodes) and call them connected.
\end{itemize}
Both visit-bounded oracles are bounded by the \emph{number of nodes visited}, the property that
distinguishes them from the distance-bounded matrix power; what differs is the \emph{order} of
exploration, and on two dense cliques on one bridge that order changes everything.

\paragraph{Depth-first dives, breadth-first gets stuck --- and that opposite trend is what
separates them.} The advisor's word was ``DFS'', loosely ``a traversal that stops early''; making
it precise forces the split above, and on this graph the two halves behave \emph{oppositely}. A
bounded \emph{DFS} \emph{dives}: from a node in the near clique it reaches the bridge endpoint
within a couple of steps and crosses to the far clique almost immediately, so it does \emph{not}
get stuck in the near clique --- its cross-block rate is high, and \emph{rises} as the budget (and
clique size) grows, because a larger budget reaches deeper into the far clique. A bounded
\emph{BFS} instead \emph{gets stuck}: it first fills the whole near clique (all $c-1$ neighbours at
distance $1$) and only crosses the bridge once the budget exceeds the near-clique size $c$ --- so
its cross-block rate is low and \emph{falls} with the clique size, however few hops away the far
clique is. This opposite trend (DFS up, BFS down) is exactly the lever the clique-size sweep uses
to decide which one the model resembles; the directional asymmetry of the single-start DFS (one
clique resolved, the other partial) is a second lever.

We score, per family, which oracle the model's mistakes resemble --- agreement on the
\emph{cross-block} entries where the matrix-power and traversal oracles disagree, not just overall
accuracy --- and how that agreement moves with the clique size and with the budget. If the model's
cross-block accuracy falls with the clique size, that already rules the depth-first oracle out (it
\emph{rises}); whether it then tracks the breadth-first oracle at a small budget, or stays flat at
$1$ like matrix power, is what decides between a visit-bounded traversal and matrix powering.
(As Section~\ref{sec:res-base} reports, the model falls, has no asymmetry, and matches the
breadth-first oracle --- so from there on we carry matrix power vs.\ bounded-BFS.)

\paragraph{Implementation (this session).} New code: the two generators in \texttt{data.py};
\texttt{dfs\_oracle.py} (matrix-power, bounded-BFS, bounded-DFS); \texttt{eval\_bridged\_cliques.py}
(discrimination, per-block, asymmetry, clique-size sweep, oracle agreement; eval-only) with
\texttt{scripts/eval\_bridged\_cliques.sbatch} over the base checkpoints; the dedicated
classifier \texttt{experiments2/train\_bridged\_classifier.py} with
\texttt{scripts/bridged\_classifier.sbatch}; the density-in-training sweep reuses

\subsection{The dedicated classifier: the bridge is detectable at every clique size}
\label{sec:res-clf}

\paragraph{Setup recap.} We train the binary classifier of Section~\ref{sec:plan-barbell}(b)
(the standard minimal trunk --- $L=2$, single head, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=512$, normalised-ReLU ---
with mean-pool $+$ one logit) from scratch at $n=20$, $200{,}000$ steps. The data is an
\emph{online stream}: there is no fixed training set, each graph is freshly generated at every
step, with the bridged/split label drawn $50/50$. A graph of ``clique size $c$'' is two cliques of
$c$ nodes (so $2c$ of the $n=20$ nodes are used, the remaining $20-2c$ left isolated), bridged or
not; $c$ can range over $\{2,3,\dots,10\}$ ($c=10$ fills the whole canvas, no padding).

The two \textbf{regimes} differ only in how $c$ is chosen for each graph of the stream, and they
ask two different questions:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.1em}
  \item \textbf{Random $c$ (the discriminator).} For \emph{every} training graph, $c$ is drawn
        uniformly from $\{2,\dots,10\}$, so a \emph{single} model sees all clique sizes mixed
        together (each $\approx 1/9$ of the stream). At evaluation we read accuracy \emph{separately
        per clique size}: does the \emph{same} model get worse as the cliques grow? Flat in $c$ is
        the matrix-powering reading, falling at large $c$ is the bounded-traversal (DFS-like)
        reading. Four seeds.
  \item \textbf{Fixed $c$ (convergence vs.\ density).} The whole stream uses a single clique size;
        we train a separate model on $c=3$, on $c=6$ and on $c=10$ (two seeds each). Here the
        question is about \emph{optimisation}, not capacity: is a denser clique slower or harder to
        learn? We compare convergence speed and loss across the three densities.
\end{itemize}
The balanced test set has $200$ graphs per label per clique size (covering $c=2,\dots,10$ in the
random regime, the single $c$ in the fixed regime).

\paragraph{Final accuracy is flat at $1.0$ across every clique size.} By the end of training all
four random-$c$ seeds reach $\approx1.00$ accuracy at \emph{every} clique size $c=2,\dots,10$
(the largest pair of cliques fills the whole $n=20$ canvas); the worst single cell over all
seeds and sizes is $0.995$. The fixed-$c$ runs tell the same story: $c=3$, $6$ and $10$ all reach
$1.00$, and in fact already at the first evaluation ($2{,}000$ steps). So there is \emph{no
permanent accuracy wall} in the clique size: the architecture can be taught to separate the two
labels however large the cliques get, which is the matrix-powering-consistent outcome and rules
out the strongest form of the bounded-traversal hypothesis (that a bridge behind a large clique is
simply \emph{undetectable}). (This classifier cannot tell bounded-DFS from bounded-BFS --- both
predict the same difficulty ordering below; that separation waits for the base model,
Section~\ref{sec:res-base}.)

\paragraph{But the bounded-traversal difficulty ordering does show up --- in the learning
dynamics.} The flat final curve hides a transient that is exactly the shape a bounded traversal
predicts. Figure~\ref{fig:clf} plots,
for each seed, the accuracy of every clique size as a function of the training step (one line per
size, colour $=$ size): the small cliques shoot up to $1.0$ almost immediately, the larger ones
only later, so the curves fan out by size before all converging --- the larger, denser cliques are
the \emph{last} to be mastered. Read as a snapshot early in training
(Table~\ref{tab:clf_early}, step $2{,}000$): seed $1000$ is $1.00$ up to $c=6$ but only $0.65$ at
$c=8$ and $0.55$ ($\approx$ chance) at $c=10$; seed $3000$ is milder ($0.87$ at $c=10$), seed
$4000$ milder still, seed $2000$ already flat. This is a bounded-traversal-like ordering that the
model overcomes with more steps, not a hard limit. The fixed-$c$ runs corroborate the optimisation
side (Figure~\ref{fig:clf_loss}): although accuracy reaches $1.0$ at every $c$, the early
($2{,}000$-step) training \emph{loss} rises with the clique size ($\approx0.05$ at $c=3$,
$\approx0.17$ at $c=6$, $\approx0.35$ at $c=10$), so a denser clique is genuinely a bit harder to
fit --- the ``density in optimisation'' effect a bounded traversal predicts --- just not hard
enough to delay reaching $100\%$.

\paragraph{Pushing the range: $n=40$, cliques up to $c=20$.} At $n=20$ the largest clique is
$c=10$, so the ``flat at $1.0$'' conclusion is only tested that far; a bounded-traversal limit, if
real, might only bite at larger cliques. We therefore re-ran the whole classifier at $n=40$, where
the clique size reaches $c=20$ (two $20$-cliques fill the entire canvas), with fixed sizes
$c\in\{5,10,15,20\}$. The conclusion is the \emph{same, only stronger}. The final accuracy is
\textbf{exactly $1.0$ at every clique size $c=2,\dots,20$, for all four seeds} (no dips at all,
cleaner than $n=20$): doubling the range does not produce a wall, so the bridge stays detectable
behind even a $20$-node dense clique --- the matrix-powering-consistent reading, and a sharper
refutation of the strong bounded-traversal hypothesis (the ``undetectable'' form).

\paragraph{The bounded-traversal cost reappears --- as an optimisation cost, more clearly than at
$n=20$.} With the larger range the difficulty ordering is now unmistakable. The per-size
trajectories (Figure~\ref{fig:clf_n40}) fan out widely: small cliques are learned almost at once,
the largest only after tens of thousands of steps. As a snapshot at step $2{,}000$
(Table~\ref{tab:clf_early_n40}) accuracy falls \emph{monotonically} with the clique size: seed
$4000$ is $1.00$ at $c=2$, $0.77$ at $c=8$ and flat at chance ($\approx0.50$) from $c\ge11$, with
seed $3000$ similar and seeds $1000$/$2000$ slower still --- the larger the clique, the later it is
mastered. The fixed-$c$ runs make the optimisation side explicit and sharp
(Figure~\ref{fig:clf_loss}): the step-$2{,}000$ training loss climbs from $\approx0.1$ at $c=5$ to
$\approx0.69$ ($=\ln 2$, i.e.\ \emph{still at chance}) at $c=15$ and $c=20$, and the step at which
accuracy first reaches $0.99$ grows from $2{,}000$ (at $c=5,10$) to $4{,}000$--$6{,}000$ (at
$c=15,20$). So a
denser/larger clique is genuinely harder and slower to learn --- exactly the ``density in
optimisation'' prediction of a bounded traversal (DFS or BFS alike) --- but, given enough steps,
never an accuracy ceiling. The bounded-traversal difficulty shows up as a \emph{training cost}, not a capability
limit. (At $n=20$, where $c\le10$, this was barely visible: every fixed-$c$ run was already at
$1.0$ by the first evaluation.)

\paragraph{Two caveats, stated plainly.} First, training is \emph{noisy} at both sizes: some seeds
suffer transient mid-run collapses to chance on the hardest cliques before settling (the step at
which all clique sizes stabilise above $0.99$ ranges from $\sim4{,}000$ to $\sim116{,}000$ across
the $n=40$ seeds), so the clique-size ordering is a tendency of the early dynamics, not a clean
monotone law --- the familiar seed lottery of Reports~III--IV, here at the level of which clique
sizes are mastered when. Second, and more important for the headline
question: this classifier is a \emph{weak} discriminator of matrix powering vs.\ a bounded
traversal. The two
labels differ by exactly one edge, and that edge leaves a purely \emph{local} footprint --- the
two bridge endpoints have degree $c$ instead of $c-1$, and the total edge count differs by one ---
so the labels are separable without ever propagating connectivity across the cliques. A flat
$1.0$ therefore shows the bridge is \emph{detectable} at any clique size; it does \emph{not} show
that the model computes the full connectivity matrix by matrix powering. That stronger question
--- whether the base connectivity model actually fills $\hat R$ correctly across the bridge, and
whether its errors carry the bounded-traversal signature --- is what the base-model eval
(Section~\ref{sec:plan-barbell}(a)) and the oracle comparison (Section~\ref{sec:plan-oracle})
decide. We turn to it now, and it gives a sharper answer than the classifier.

\subsection{The base model: the single bridge is not propagated across a large clique}
\label{sec:res-base}

\paragraph{Setup recap.} This is the decisive test (Section~\ref{sec:plan-barbell}(a)). We take
the base connectivity model of Reports~III--IV --- the RoBERTa-faithful transformer with the
\emph{linear} read-out ($L=2$, single head, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=512$), the model that emits the
full $n\times n$ connectivity matrix $\hat R$ --- and feed it the bridged and split cliques
\emph{without any further training} (eval-only). We use both the ER-trained and the mixed-trained
checkpoints, at $n=20$ and $n=40$, four seeds each --- eight for the $n=20$ mixed model
($20$ checkpoints in all). (As a consistency check, independently retrained copies of the four
$n=20$/$n=40$ base checkpoints reproduce every block accuracy below to two decimals on each seed
--- \texttt{bridged\_cliques\_xcheck/}, so the result is not an artefact of one particular set of
weights.) The \textbf{mixed} model is
the clean test: dense cliques (2cliques, clique-blocks) are in its training stream, so it is
\emph{not} out-of-distribution on the cliques themselves; the \textbf{ER} model has only seen
sparse graphs, so on dense cliques it is in the degree-heuristic OOD regime of Report~IV
(within-clique accuracy already $<1$) and we read it only as a confounded cross-check, not as
evidence on its own (Report~IV, Thread~1). For each bridged graph we split $\hat R$ into the
left-clique block, the right-clique block and the cross block, and we sweep the clique size
$c$ (so $2c$ nodes are used, the rest left isolated). The bridge always sits at distance $\le 3$,
\emph{at every $c$} --- so matrix powering must score the cross block $1.0$ for all $c$, while a
visit-bounded traversal must lose it once one clique alone exhausts the budget.

\paragraph{What ``cross-block pair accuracy'' means (and why it is the quantity that matters).}
The model outputs an $n\times n$ matrix $\hat R$; entry $\hat R_{ij}$ is its guess for ``are nodes
$i$ and $j$ in the same component?'' A \emph{pair} is one such $(i,j)$ entry, and \emph{pair
accuracy} on a set of pairs is just the fraction of those entries the model gets right (predicted
$0/1$ equals the true $0/1$). We group the pairs into three blocks: \emph{within-A} (both nodes in
the left clique), \emph{within-B} (both in the right clique), and \emph{cross} (one node in each
clique). On a \emph{bridged} graph the two cliques are one component, so the true label of
\emph{every} cross pair is $1$ (connected); cross-block accuracy is therefore the fraction of A--B
pairs the model correctly calls connected, and $\approx 0.00$ means it calls essentially all of
them \emph{disconnected}. The key thing to keep separate: the bridge is a single \emph{edge}, and
it lives in the \emph{input} adjacency $A$ (the model can see it); the cross \emph{block} lives in
the \emph{output} $\hat R$ and has $c^2$ entries. Filling it correctly does not mean copying one
edge --- it means \emph{propagating} that one edge into ``and therefore all $c^2$ cross pairs are
connected.'' Seeing the edge and propagating it are different operations, and that distinction is
the whole story below.

\paragraph{Within each clique: perfect. Across the bridge: collapses with clique size.} The mixed
model resolves each clique \emph{perfectly} and reports two components on the split graph
\emph{perfectly} --- within-A, within-B and the whole split matrix are all exactly $1.00$, every
seed, both $n$ (Table~\ref{tab:base_blocks}). So the failure is \emph{not} that dense cliques are
hard (they are in-distribution; the model gets them right). The only thing the mixed model misses
is the \emph{cross} block of the bridged graph: at full clique size ($c=10$ at $n=20$, $c=20$ at
$n=40$) the cross-block accuracy is $\approx 0.00$ --- it sees the two bridged cliques as
\emph{two} components, exactly as if the bridge were not there. And the collapse is graded in the
clique size (Figure~\ref{fig:base_sweep}, Table~\ref{tab:base_sweep}): cross accuracy is flat at
$1.0$ for small cliques and falls to $0$ as the cliques grow ($n=20$: $1.0$ up to $c=6$, then
$0.27$ at $c=7$, $0.01$ at $c=10$; $n=40$: $1.0$ up to $c=6$, $0.74$ at $c=7$, $0.27$ at $c=8$,
$0$ from $c\simeq14$). The matrix-power oracle, on the same graphs, is flat at $1.0$ for every $c$
(the bridge is $\le 3$ hops). \textbf{The model departs from matrix powering exactly where a
bounded traversal would: once the near clique alone is too big to ``get through'' before reaching
the far one.}

\paragraph{The cutoff is an absolute node budget, not a fraction of the graph.} The collapse
happens at roughly the \emph{same absolute clique size} at $n=20$ (knee at $c\approx6$--$7$) and
$n=40$ (knee at $c\approx7$--$8$), even though $c=7$ is $70\%$ of the $n=20$ canvas but only $35\%$
of the $n=40$ one. A traversal that can cover about $6$--$7$ nodes before running out of budget
explains both. That is precisely the quantity the DFS hypothesis says bounds the model --- the
\emph{number of nodes traversed} --- and \emph{not} the shortest-path distance, which is $\le 3$
at every $c$ and would predict no collapse at all.

\paragraph{The two visit-bounded references make opposite predictions here --- and bounded-DFS is
rejected twice over.} This is the experiment that separates a bounded \emph{depth}-first search
from a bounded \emph{breadth}-first one, and the data picks the second on two independent grounds.
\emph{(i) Trend with clique size} (Figure~\ref{fig:base_sweep}, the two extra oracle curves). A
bounded DFS \emph{dives}: from a node in the near clique it reaches the bridge in a step or two and
crosses, so a \emph{bigger} budget lets it reach \emph{deeper} into the far clique --- its
cross-block accuracy \emph{rises} with the clique size ($0.19\to0.62$ at $n=20$, $\to0.69$ at
$n=40$, orange). The model does the \emph{opposite}: it \emph{falls} from $1$ to $0$. A bounded BFS
fills the near clique first and only crosses once the budget exceeds it, so its cross accuracy is
low and falling (red) --- the same direction as the model. So the model is \emph{not} a depth-first
search; its trend matches the breadth-first ball. \emph{(ii) Asymmetry.} A single-start DFS also
predicts a \emph{directional} signature (Table~\ref{tab:predict}): the start clique fully resolved,
the far one only partial, $\hat R_{ij}\neq\hat R_{ji}$. We see none --- both cliques are resolved
equally ($1.00$ vs $1.00$), both cross directions fail equally
($\hat R_{AB}\approx\hat R_{BA}\approx0$), and the directional-disagreement rate is $\approx0$. The
reason is structural: a transformer emits all of $\hat R$ \emph{in parallel}, so there is no single
start node and no preferred direction. Both checks point to the same reference, the
\emph{visit-bounded BFS ball} we anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:plan-oracle}: every node resolves
the budget-many nearest nodes (its own clique, exactly right) and drops the bridge into the far
clique once the near clique alone fills the budget. We therefore set the depth-first reading aside
and carry the contrast \emph{matrix powering vs bounded-BFS} forward (it is also the contrast the
natural-families capstone uses).

\paragraph{How the oracle comparison works (and why the oracles are not ``always right'').} The two
oracles are \emph{not} scored for correctness --- they are two \emph{reference algorithms}, and we
ask which one the model's answers (mistakes included) look like. One of them, the \emph{matrix-power
oracle} $\mathbf 1[(A+I)^{9}>0]$, does happen to be the true connectivity, so on these graphs it is
right everywhere (cross block $=1$). The other, the \emph{visit-bounded BFS} with a fixed budget, is
\emph{deliberately} an early-stopping algorithm: it explores a ball of the nearest budget-many nodes
and calls only those connected, so on the bridged cliques it gets the cross block \emph{wrong} on
purpose (it never leaves the near clique). They are useful precisely because they \emph{disagree},
and exactly on the cross block. So the comparison is: take the model's $0/1$ for each pair and ask
what fraction matches each oracle's $0/1$. ``Overall agreement with the matrix-power oracle is only
$0.48$'' means the model's matrix matches the \emph{true} connectivity on only $48\%$ of pairs ---
which is just the within-block pairs; it disagrees with the truth on essentially the whole cross
block. The visit-bounded BFS, on the other hand, the model matches almost perfectly: at $n=20$,
with the budget set to one clique ($b=c=10$), the model agrees with the BFS oracle on $99\%$ of all
pairs, and on the contested cross entries --- the pairs where the two oracles split --- it sides
with the BFS oracle $\approx 99.4\%$ of the time and with matrix powering $0.6\%$
(Figure~\ref{fig:base_oracle}; identical across the eight seeds, and the same direction at $n=40$,
where it reaches $\sim\!79\%$ BFS on the contested entries at $b=10$ and keeps climbing toward the
clique size). One honest refinement, visible in the clique-size sweep
(Figure~\ref{fig:base_sweep}) rather than in this single-$c$ scan: at \emph{small} cliques the
model is genuinely \emph{matrix power} (cross block $=1.0$, where the BFS oracle is already near
$0$), not BFS. The fuller statement is therefore that the model computes connectivity correctly
\emph{up to a node budget} of $\sim6$--$7$ --- where it equals matrix power --- and collapses to the
stuck-BFS floor beyond it. That is exactly a \emph{visit-bounded} computation: matrix power within
the budget, a stalled traversal past it. The agreement numbers above are measured at the full
clique size, where the budget is already exceeded and the model has settled onto the BFS branch.

\paragraph{The ER model is the confounded cross-check (do not read it alone).} The ER curves
(Figure~\ref{fig:base_sweep}, bottom) fall with $c$ too, but for a mixture of reasons we cannot
disentangle (Table~\ref{tab:base_er}): the ER model is already wrong \emph{within} the cliques
(degree-heuristic OOD, within-clique accuracy $0.0$--$1.0$ depending on the seed), and it is far
noisier seed-to-seed
(one $n{=}40$ seed collapses to $0$ everywhere; another rises back toward $1$ at large $c$ only
because the degree heuristic calls everything dense ``connected'' --- it then \emph{also} wrongly
calls the \emph{split} cliques connected, so its discrimination stays at chance). A falling ER
curve is therefore not clean evidence of bounded traversal --- density-OOD and bridge-distance
grow together (Report~IV, error noted in our handoff). This is exactly why the mixed model is the
test that counts, and there the within-clique blocks are perfect, so the cross collapse is
attributable to the bridge alone.

\paragraph{Why this does not contradict the classifier (cross block $\approx 0$ here, accuracy
$1.0$ there).} This is the natural objection, so it is worth spelling out: in
Section~\ref{sec:res-clf} the classifier reached $1.0$ at every clique size, and here the same kind
of model scores $0.00$ on the same bridged graph. There is no contradiction, because the two models
are asked \emph{different questions}. The classifier emits \emph{one bit} --- ``is there a bridge,
yes or no?'' --- and that bit is readable from a purely \emph{local} fingerprint of the bridge edge:
the two endpoints have degree $c$ instead of $c-1$, and the graph has one more edge in total. You
can tell bridged from split by counting, without ever working out who is connected to whom. (This is
the weak-discriminator caveat of Section~\ref{sec:res-clf}: detecting the edge is easy.) The base
model is asked the \emph{harder} question --- fill in all $c^2$ cross pairs of $\hat R$ --- and that
requires \emph{propagating} the single bridge edge into ``therefore every node on the left is
connected to every node on the right.'' It is this propagation, not the detection, that fails:
the model plainly \emph{sees} the bridge edge (it is right there in the input $A$, and the classifier
proves it is usable), but it does \emph{not} carry that connection across a clique of more than
$\sim 6$ nodes when it builds the connectivity matrix. So the user's intuition --- ``can't it just
read off the $1$ at the bridge position?'' --- is exactly what the classifier does; the base model's
job is the opposite of reading off one entry, it is spreading that one entry over the whole cross
block, and that is where the bounded traversal runs out. On the decisive read --- a
model that already handles the dense cliques, failing the bridge in a graded, absolute-node-budget
way that tracks a visit-bounded traversal and parts from the matrix-power oracle --- the base-model
eval comes down on the side of a \emph{bounded-traversal} mechanism, not distance-bounded matrix
powering.

\subsection{Density in optimisation: denser training is faster, not slower}
\label{sec:res-density}

\paragraph{Setup recap.} This is the training-side test of Section~\ref{sec:plan-density}. We
train the base connectivity model --- the same RoBERTa-faithful trunk with the \emph{linear}
read-out ($L=2$, single head, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=512$) --- from scratch on a pure ER stream at
$n=20$, for $1{,}000{,}000$ steps, four seeds, sweeping the edge probability
$p\in\{0.05,0.08,0.12,0.16,0.22\}$ (mean degree $\approx 1.0,\,1.6,\,2.4,\,3.2,\,4.4$; the
$n=20$ connectivity threshold $\ln 20/20\approx 0.15$ sits inside the sweep). Nothing else
changes between runs. This experiment does \emph{not} separate the two visit-bounded algorithms
from each other --- \emph{both} bounded-DFS and bounded-BFS are bounded by the amount of structure
to traverse, so both predict a \emph{denser} stream should be \emph{harder / slower} to fit. What
it separates is the visit-bounded family as a whole from matrix powering, which predicts the
\emph{opposite} (denser graphs have shorter distances, so connectivity is easier). We read two
things off each run: how fast and how reliably the in-distribution exact match converges, and
how well the trained model transfers out of distribution.

\paragraph{Denser training converges faster and more reliably --- the matrix-powering
direction.} Figure~\ref{fig:dens_conv} and Table~\ref{tab:dens_conv} are unambiguous: as the
training density rises, the model reaches a given in-distribution accuracy in \emph{fewer}
steps, not more. The number of steps to $0.99$ exact match drops from $\sim\!10^5$ at $p=0.05$
to $\sim\!7\cdot10^4$ at $p=0.12$ and to $\sim\!5{,}000$ (the very first evaluation) at
$p\ge0.16$; the accuracy at the first eval ($5{,}000$ steps) climbs monotonically with $p$
($0.71\to0.57\to0.93\to0.99\to0.999$). Reliability moves the same way: at the sparse end some
seeds never finish converging (one of four at $p=0.05$, two of four at $p=0.08$ plateau at
$\approx0.95$--$0.98$, never crossing $0.99$ --- the familiar seed lottery of Reports~I--III),
whereas every seed at $p\ge0.12$ reaches exactly $1.00$. So denser training is both faster and
less of a coin-flip. This is the \emph{matrix-powering}-consistent outcome and the opposite of
what the strong ``density hurts optimisation'' form of \emph{either} visit-bounded hypothesis
(DFS or BFS) predicts.

\paragraph{The honest caveat: ER conflates density with distance, so this is not a clean probe
of the traversal axis.} Two things must be said plainly. First, the very sparse end is
non-monotone: $p=0.08$ is actually the \emph{noisiest} setting (worst seed lottery), not
$p=0.05$, because near the connectivity threshold the target matrix $R$ is a genuine mix of
connected and disconnected pairs (hardest to fit), while at $p=0.05$ it is almost all-zeros and
at $p\ge0.16$ almost all-ones (both trivial). Second, and more important: in an ER graph,
raising the density \emph{shortens distances} at the same time as it adds edges, and beyond
$p\approx0.16$ the in-distribution task is essentially trivial (the graph is one component, $R$
is all-ones, the first eval is already at ceiling --- the saturation we flagged in advance). So
``denser $=$ faster'' here is largely the connectivity \emph{task} getting easier (shorter
distances, simpler target), \emph{not} a clean measurement of the quantity a bounded traversal
(DFS or BFS) is sensitive to.
The ER sweep cannot isolate ``more nodes to traverse at fixed distance,'' because in ER more
edges means \emph{shorter} distance, the reverse coupling. That clean isolation is exactly what
the bridged-clique experiments do (bridge fixed at $\le3$ hops, only the clique size grows), and
there --- Sections~\ref{sec:res-clf}--\ref{sec:res-base} --- the bounded-traversal cost
\emph{does} appear (the classifier's transient slowdown with clique size, the base model's
cross-block collapse). The two results are consistent: when density and distance move together
(ER), the distance effect wins and denser is easier; when they are decoupled (cliques), the
traversal-cost effect surfaces. The ER sweep therefore rules out the strong claim that dense
\emph{training} is simply harder for \emph{any} visit-bounded traversal, without touching the
bounded-BFS evidence from the clique tests.

\paragraph{OOD transfer peaks just below the connectivity threshold.} The more interesting
read is the out-of-distribution transfer (Figure~\ref{fig:dens_ood}, Table~\ref{tab:dens_ood}).
On the long, sparse structured families held out of this stream (1cycle, path-union, chain-plus),
exact match is \emph{non-monotone} in $p$, with a clear peak at $p=0.12$ --- just below the
connectivity threshold --- and a collapse on either side. Too sparse ($p=0.05$--$0.08$): transfer
is a seed lottery (only the one or two seeds that fully converge transfer at all; the rest are
near $0$). Too dense ($p\ge0.16$): transfer collapses across all seeds, because a model trained on
short, dense graphs never practises long-range reach, so it fails the long structured graphs ---
$p=0.22$ gives $\approx0$ on every held-out family. The sweet spot at $p=0.12$ is also the
\emph{least} seed-dependent (all four seeds agree: 1cycle $=1.00$, path-union $\approx0.73$,
chain-plus $\approx0.37$), because at that density the training graphs span the widest useful
range of distances. This is a small but clean ``don't train too dense'' lesson, and it is
consistent with the distance reading: dense training narrows the distances seen and costs the
model its long-range reach.

\subsection{Oracle agreement across the natural families (capstone)}
\label{sec:res-capstone}
% SOURCE: eval_oracle_agreement_families.py via scripts/oracle_agreement_families.sbatch
%   (HPC job 533393). Output: runs/report5/oracle_families/<tag>/oracle_families.json
%   (per-family + "pooled"); aggregate with plot_oracle_families.py.
% WHAT IT TESTS: sec 5.2 showed (on the bridged cliques only) the model tracks a
%   visit-bounded BFS, not matrix power. This generalises that to ALL natural families
%   (n20/n40, ER/mixed): for each checkpoint, agreement of R-hat with the matrix-power
%   oracle vs the bounded-BFS oracle (budget sweep), restricted to the CROSS pairs where
%   the two oracles DISAGREE (json field disagree_frac) -- NOT overall agreement.
% PREDICTION: if the model is visit-bounded, on the families where the two oracles split
%   it should side with bounded-BFS, more so on DENSE families (clique_blocks, er_blocks)
%   and at n40. MP-vs-BFS in the main figure; the DFS oracle is re-added (oracle_families_dfs/,
%   job oraclefamdfs) to confirm DFS is rejected on the natural barbell too -- NOT assumed dead.
% HONEST CAVEAT (groundwork runs/report5/report5_figs/oracle_disagreement.{png,json}):
%   on SPARSE families at n20 the two oracles nearly coincide (disagree_frac ~0.01-0.10)
%   -> the test is BLIND there (distance ~ nodes-traversed). Discriminating power is in
%   the DENSE families and at n40. Report disagree_frac, say where it is too small to read.
% CAPTION (rule 21): Model = base RoBERTa linear (L=2, 1 head, d512), mixed- and ER-trained,
%   4 seeds, n20/n40. Test = the natural families (#graphs each). Metric = fraction of the
%   oracle-disagreement cross pairs the model matches BFS vs matrix-power (mean over seeds).
\paragraph{Setup recap.} Section~\ref{sec:res-base} showed, \emph{on the bridged cliques}, that
the base model tracks a visit-bounded BFS rather than matrix power. That graph was purpose-built
to decouple distance from nodes-traversed. The capstone asks whether the same signature is visible
on the \emph{natural} families of Reports~III--IV. We take every base checkpoint (RoBERTa,
\emph{linear} read-out, $L=2$, single head, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=512$; mixed- and ER-trained, four
seeds, $n=20$ and $n=40$ --- the same $16$ checkpoints as Section~\ref{sec:res-base}) and, for each
of the $12$ families, compare its matrix $\hat R$ to the three reference oracles of
Section~\ref{sec:plan-oracle}: the matrix-power oracle $\mathbf 1[(A+I)^{9}>0]$ and the two
visit-bounded traversals (bounded-BFS and bounded-DFS) as their budget $b$ (nodes visited) is swept
from $2$ to $n$. The quantity that matters is \emph{not} overall agreement but, restricted to the
pairs where matrix power and a given traversal \emph{disagree} (the \texttt{disagree\_frac} mass),
the fraction the model matches each one. The figures below report matrix-power vs.\ \emph{both}
bounded traversals, so the natural-family test is run against BFS and DFS alike. We do not treat the
depth-first oracle as settled from Section~\ref{sec:res-base} alone: its rejection
in Section~\ref{sec:res-base} rests on the \emph{constructed} bridged cliques, and the capstone now
shows (next paragraphs) that the only \emph{natural} family carrying that same bridge-behind-a-dense-
clique structure --- and the only one where a visit-bounded signature appears at all --- is the
barbell. It would be circular to declare DFS dead on the strength of the one construction and then
not check the one natural graph that reproduces it. We have therefore re-run this same capstone with
the bounded-\emph{DFS} oracle added back (\texttt{eval\_oracle\_agreement\_families.py} now scores all
three oracles, including the direct BFS-vs-DFS contrast, into
\texttt{runs/report5/oracle\_families\_dfs/}). That re-run has now landed, and on the barbell ---
restricted to the cross pairs where the BFS and DFS oracles \emph{themselves} disagree, the direct
separator --- the breadth-first reading holds where the test has power: at $n=40$ (larger cliques, a
disagreement mass up to $\approx0.28$) the mixed model sides with the \emph{breadth}-first oracle on
$0.61$--$0.74$ of those pairs across the discriminating budgets ($b=8$--$20$) and with the
\emph{depth}-first one on only $0.26$--$0.37$. At $n=20$ the separation is too weak to call
(mass-weighted follows-BFS $\approx0.54$, a near toss-up that even leans depth-first at the single
peak-mass budget), and the dense single-clique families (2cliques, clique-blocks) produce \emph{zero}
BFS--DFS disagreement, so they cannot separate the two at all. So where the natural-graph test retains
any power --- the $n=40$ barbell --- it reconfirms the breadth-first, not depth-first, reading of
Section~\ref{sec:res-base}, and the depth-first rejection no longer rests on the constructed cliques
alone.

\paragraph{Reading the budget axis (and a trap to avoid).} The bounded-BFS budget is a property of
the \emph{oracle}, not the model. At a \emph{large} budget $b\to n$ the BFS ball visits the whole
component, so BFS$(b)$ \emph{becomes} the true connectivity; ``the model follows BFS'' there means
only that the model correctly connects beyond-capacity pairs --- the Report~IV recovery past the
$3^L$ wall --- and is no evidence of a bounded traversal. The $\S$\ref{sec:res-base} signature is
the \emph{opposite}: the model matching a \emph{stuck, small-budget} ball on a connection that has
to propagate across many nodes. So the discriminating region is \emph{small} $b$ together with a
large disagreement mass. (We also drop the two smallest budgets $b=2,3$: a per-seed fraction there
exceeds $1$, a numerical artefact, harmless once pooled but excluded to be safe.)

\paragraph{On the natural families the model looks like matrix power, not a bounded traversal ---
neither breadth- nor depth-first.} Figure~\ref{fig:capstone} and Table~\ref{tab:capstone} give the
pooled answer over all $12$ families, against \emph{both} bounded traversals at once (the figure now
carries three curves: follows matrix-power, follows bounded-BFS, follows bounded-DFS --- the last
added by the DFS-inclusive re-run). It is clean. For the \textbf{mixed} model, in the region where
the comparison has power --- small budget ($b\le 9$), where matrix power genuinely disagrees with
the traversal oracles on $19$--$52\%$ of pairs --- the model follows \emph{matrix power} on
$95$--$98\%$ of the contested pairs, the BFS oracle on $\le 5\%$ \emph{and} the DFS oracle on
$\le 6\%$ (both $n$; the BFS- and DFS-following curves sit on top of each other, near zero). So the
DFS oracle is matched no better than the BFS one: pooled over the natural families the model is not a
bounded traversal of \emph{either} kind. Both traversal curves only climb past $0.5$ at large budget
--- $b\approx 16$ ($n=20$), $b\approx 32$ ($n=40$) --- and exactly there the disagreement mass has
collapsed (to $\approx 0.06$ at $n=20$) and the bounded oracle has all but turned into the true
connectivity, so that late rise is the beyond-wall recovery (Report~IV), not a stuck ball. Pooled
over the everyday families the base model is observationally \emph{matrix power}: the visit-bounded
signature of Section~\ref{sec:res-base} does \emph{not} reappear here, for BFS or DFS.

\paragraph{Why this does not contradict Section~\ref{sec:res-base} --- it locates the signature.}
This is the expected outcome given the premise of the whole report (Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}):
on a natural graph distance and nodes-traversed move together (Report~IV, $\mathrm{corr}=-0.82$), so
the matrix-power and BFS oracles disagree only on (i) \emph{short} within-component pairs at small
$b$ --- where the model is correctly matrix-power --- and (ii) \emph{far} pairs at large $b$ --- where
BFS has become the truth. Neither tests ``propagate one connection across a large dense block,''
which is exactly what the bridged construction isolates and the natural families cannot. The lone
exception is the \textbf{barbell} --- the one held-out natural family that, like the bridged cliques,
puts a thin bridge between two dense regions --- and it behaves accordingly: in the discriminating
mid-budget range the mixed model leans toward bounded-BFS ($n=40$: follows-BFS $0.29\to0.49$ across
$b=2$--$14$; $n=20$: $0.21\to0.58$ across $b=6$--$12$), the natural-graph echo of
Section~\ref{sec:res-base}. The \textbf{ER} model is again the confounded cross-check
(Table~\ref{tab:base_er}, error noted in our handoff): being degree-heuristic OOD on dense families
it is already wrong \emph{inside} the clusters, which makes it look more BFS-like ($\approx0.25$
even at small $b$) for the wrong reason; we do not read it as evidence.

\paragraph{Bottom line of the capstone.} The bounded-traversal mechanism is real but \emph{visible
only on graphs built to expose it} (the bridged cliques of Section~\ref{sec:res-base}, and the
barbell). On the families one meets in the wild the distance/node collinearity masks it, and where
the test retains any power the model is matrix-power. The capstone therefore does not extend the
visit-bounded reading to all graphs; it shows \emph{where} that reading does and does not hold, and
why. The one step that was still open before the verdict --- because the barbell is the single natural
family that shows the bounded signature, \emph{which} bounded traversal it resembles, the stalling
breadth-first ball or the diving depth-first walk --- is now answered by the DFS-inclusive re-run above
(\texttt{oracle\_families\_dfs}): on the $n=40$ barbell, the only natural setting where the contrast has
power, the model tracks the breadth-first oracle, not the depth-first one. The depth-first reading is
therefore rejected not only on the constructed bridged cliques but on the one natural graph that
reproduces them; matrix power vs.\ bounded-BFS is the contrast we carry into the verdict.

\subsection{Does the node budget scale with the read-out?}
\label{sec:res-sim}
% SOURCE: eval_bridged_cliques.py (eval-only) via scripts/eval_bridged_similarity.sbatch
%   on the EXISTING similarity checkpoints (runs/families_n{20,40}/..._similarity_lam0_seed*).
%   Output: runs/report5/bridged_similarity/<tag>/bridged_cliques.json.
%   NB: plot_bridged_cliques.py must be extended to read this dir.
% WHAT IT TESTS: Report IV showed the similarity read-out DOUBLES the distance reach
%   (3^L -> 2*3^L). If the clique-size node budget of sec 5.2 (~6-7) is the SAME capacity,
%   the bridged cross-block knee should move from ~6-7 to ~13-14 under similarity.
% PREDICTION: knee doubles -> budget and distance-reach are the same lever (ties 5.2 to
%   Report IV). Knee unchanged -> they are different mechanisms (also informative).
% CAPTION (rule 21): Model = base RoBERTa SIMILARITY read-out (L=2, 1 head, d512),
%   mixed-trained (clean) + ER (cross-check), 8 seeds n20 / 4 seeds n40. Test = bridged
%   cliques, >=400 graphs per clique size. Metric = cross-block pair accuracy vs clique
%   size (mean over seeds), against the matrix-power and bounded-BFS oracles.
\paragraph{Setup recap.} Section~\ref{sec:res-base} found that the base model carries the
bridge across the cliques only \emph{up to a node budget} of $\sim6$--$7$, and beyond that the
cross block collapses. That budget is a number of \emph{nodes}, not a distance, and the obvious
follow-up question is: where does that number come from? Report~IV gives one clean handle. The
\emph{similarity} read-out --- $\hat R_{ij}=\mathrm{scale}\cdot\cos(h_i,h_j)+\mathrm{bias}$
instead of a linear map on the pair --- doubles the \emph{distance} reach there, from $3^L$ to
$2\cdot3^L$, because asking ``do the two nodes' $3^L$-hop neighbourhoods \emph{overlap}'' is a
meet-in-the-middle that needs each node to reach only halfway. If the clique-size budget of
Section~\ref{sec:res-base} is the \emph{same} capacity seen in a different geometry, then swapping
the read-out should move the bridged-clique knee the same way --- from $\sim6$--$7$ to
$\sim13$--$14$. If the budget instead stays put, the node budget and the distance reach are
\emph{different} mechanisms (also worth knowing). We test this without training anything new: we
take the existing similarity checkpoints of Report~IV (\texttt{runs/families\_n\{20,40\}/}
$\ldots$\texttt{similarity}$\ldots$) and run the identical bridged-clique eval of
Section~\ref{sec:res-base} on them --- eight seeds at $n=20$, four at $n=40$, mixed-trained (the
clean test) plus the ER cross-check.

\paragraph{The similarity read-out does not fix the bridge --- it moves the budget.} Two facts,
in order. First, at the \emph{full} clique size the similarity model fails the bridge exactly as
the linear one did: the cross block is $0.00$, every one of the eight ($n=20$) / four ($n=40$)
seeds, while each clique is still resolved perfectly (within-A $=$ within-B $=1.00$) and the split
graph is exact ($1.00$); discrimination is at chance ($0.50$), the model calling bridged and split
alike (Table~\ref{tab:sim_blocks}). So the similarity read-out does \emph{not} remove the
bounded-traversal limit --- the same wall is there. Second, and the point of the experiment: the
wall has \emph{moved out}. On the $n=40$ sweep, where the canvas has room to grow the cliques, the
cross-block knee shifts from $c\approx7$ under the linear read-out to $c\approx11$--$12$ under
similarity (Figure~\ref{fig:simknee}, Table~\ref{tab:sim_sweep}): the similarity model is still a
flat $1.00$ at $c=8$ (linear is already $0.27$ there) and only crosses $0.5$ between $c=11$ and
$c=12$. The node budget grew by roughly $4$--$5$ nodes --- in the direction of the predicted
doubling, if a little short of a clean $\times2$.

\paragraph{Why the doubling ties Section~\ref{sec:res-base} to Report~IV.} This is the causal link
we were after. The linear read-out asks a \emph{one-sided} question --- is node $j$ inside node
$i$'s aggregated neighbourhood? --- so node $i$ alone must reach all the way across the near clique
to the far one, and it runs out at $\sim6$--$7$ nodes. The similarity read-out asks a
\emph{two-sided} one --- do $i$'s and $j$'s neighbourhoods overlap? --- so each node need only reach
the bridge from its own side, and the reachable clique size roughly doubles. This is the \emph{same}
meet-in-the-middle that doubles the distance reach in Report~IV; that one architectural change lifts
\emph{both} the distance reach and the clique-size node budget tells us they are two geometries of
\emph{one} capacity, not two unrelated limits. It also says something about \emph{where} that
capacity lives: a quantity that an \emph{output} read-out (a cosine vs.\ a linear map, downstream of
the trunk) can enlarge is a property of \emph{how far the trunk's neighbourhood aggregation reaches},
which is precisely the matrix-power picture --- a fixed-depth, parallel aggregation with a budget ---
and \emph{not} a fixed step-count of a sequential traversal, which an output map could not touch. The
failure also stays perfectly symmetric (directional disagreement $0.000$ at every seed), as it must:
$\cos$ is symmetric by construction, so similarity cannot show the DFS asymmetry --- but the linear
read-out, which \emph{could}, already showed none (Section~\ref{sec:res-base}).

\paragraph{Two honest caveats.} First, the budget moves cleanly only at $n=40$. At $n=20$ the
largest clique is $c=10$ and the two cliques fill the whole canvas, so a budget of $\sim12$--$14$
simply cannot be exercised; there the similarity knee sits at $c\approx6$--$7$ like the linear one
(if anything the collapse past it is \emph{sharper} --- straight to $0.00$ by $c=8$), the same
``too small at $n=20$, the wall returns at $n=40$'' pattern the distance wall shows throughout
Reports~III--IV. The clean read is the $n=40$ mixed model. Second, the shift is $\sim\times1.5$, not
exactly $\times2$; part of that is that at the largest $n=40$ cliques ($c\to20$) the cliques again
fill the canvas, so the very top of the sweep is canvas-limited too. The conclusion we draw is the
\emph{direction and the link} --- the read-out moves the node budget, the same lever that moves the
distance reach --- not a precise multiplier. The \textbf{ER} model is, as everywhere in this report,
the confounded cross-check and must not be read as evidence: under the similarity read-out the ER
model calls the cross block connected at \emph{every} clique size (cross $=1.00$, flat), but for the
wrong reason --- it over-connects everything dense (the degree heuristic of Report~IV), so it
\emph{also} calls the \emph{split} cliques connected, leaving its split exact-match at $0.00$ and its
discrimination at chance ($0.50$); a flat-$1.00$ cross curve here is the over-connection artefact, not
a propagated bridge (Table~\ref{tab:sim_blocks}, bottom).

\subsection{Capacity or data prior? Adding the bridged cliques to the training stream}
\label{sec:res-capacity}
% SOURCE (Exp 2, data axis): train_families_n20.py --include_bridged via
%   scripts/train_bridged_in_stream.sbatch (bridged+split ADDED to the mixed stream,
%   random clique size). Checkpoints: runs/report5/train_bridged_in_stream/
%   n{N}_mixedbr_roberta_linear_lam0_seed*; auto-eval -> runs/report5/bridged_cliques_trained/.

%   scripts/train_bridged_depth_sweep.sbatch (mixed, bridged HELD OUT, L=1 and L=3;
%   L=2 = the existing base). Checkpoints: runs/report5/depth_sweep/
%   n{N}_mixed_roberta_linear_lam0_L{1,3}_seed*; auto-eval -> runs/report5/bridged_cliques_depth/.
% WHAT THEY TEST: the main caveat -- is the cross-block collapse of sec 5.2 a hard
%   CAPACITY limit at L=2, or just a DATA gap (bridged cliques were held out)?
% PREDICTIONS:
%   Exp 2: cross-block STILL collapses with clique size after training on bridged
%     -> hard visit-bounded capacity (strong conclusion). It learns to propagate at all c
%     -> it was a data gap (revise the verdict).
%   Exp 1: the clique-size knee MOVES with depth L (e.g. roughly tracks 3^L)
%     -> the budget IS the architectural capacity, same limit as the distance-9 wall, and
%     the data-prior reading is killed. Knee stays ~6-7 at every L -> NOT the 3^L capacity
%     (a surprise worth reporting).
% CAPTION (rule 21): Model = base RoBERTa linear; Exp 2 trained on mixed+bridged
%   (fam_tag mixedbr), Exp 1 trained on mixed at L in {1,2,3}; seeds + n as launched.
%   Test = bridged cliques (>=400 per clique size). Metric = cross-block pair accuracy
%   vs clique size (mean over seeds), vs the matrix-power and bounded-BFS oracles.
\paragraph{The one caveat that the rest of the report cannot remove on its own.} Everything so far
is an \emph{eval-only} read on the base model of Reports~III--IV: the bridged and split cliques were
held \emph{out} of training (the nine-family stream of Section~\ref{sec:plan-oracle} never contains a
clique joined by a single bridge). The cross-block collapse of Section~\ref{sec:res-base} --- the
whole bounded-traversal signature --- could therefore mean two very different things. It could be a
\emph{hard capacity} limit: the $L=2$ trunk simply cannot carry a single edge across more than
$\sim6$--$7$ nodes when it builds $\hat R$, no matter what it is trained on. Or it could be a mere
\emph{data gap}: the trunk \emph{can} do it, but never had a reason to learn to, because nothing in
its training distribution rewarded propagating one bridge across a dense block. These have opposite
consequences for the verdict, and only one experiment tells them apart --- \emph{put the structure in
the training stream and retrain}.

\paragraph{The test, and why the geometry makes it sharp.} We retrain the same base connectivity
model (RoBERTa-faithful trunk, linear read-out, $L=2$, single head, $d_{\mathrm{model}}=512$) on the
identical nine-family mixed stream \emph{plus} bridged and split cliques at a random clique size every
batch (the \texttt{-{}-include\_bridged} stream, fam-tag \texttt{mixedbr}); four seeds at $n=20$ and
four at $n=40$, the same $10^6$-step budget as the base model. Then we re-run the bridged probe of
Section~\ref{sec:res-base} on the retrained weights. The key geometric fact, already used in
Section~\ref{sec:res-base}, is what makes this decisive: \emph{in a bridged-cliques graph the two
bridge endpoints are one hop apart, and every cross pair is at distance $\le 3$ --- at every clique
size}. So matrix powering (capacity $3^L=9$) is \emph{capable} of the cross block for all $c$;
nothing about the distance ever exceeds the architecture. If retraining makes the collapse vanish,
the limit of Section~\ref{sec:res-base} was never the architecture --- it was the data. If the
collapse survives retraining, then there is a genuine $L=2$ bottleneck even on a within-capacity
target, and the bounded-traversal reading is structural.

\paragraph{Result: the collapse vanishes completely.} With the bridged cliques in the stream, the
cross block is propagated \emph{perfectly at every clique size}, every seed, both canvas sizes
(Figure~\ref{fig:capacity}, Table~\ref{tab:capacity}). The clique-size sweep, which fell from $1$ to
$0$ for the held-out model, is now flat at $1.00$ all the way to the largest clique that fits ($c=10$
at $n=20$, $c=20$ at $n=40$) --- it lies exactly on the matrix-power oracle, with no knee at all. The
$\sim6$--$7$ node budget of Section~\ref{sec:res-base} is simply gone. And the retrained model has
\emph{not} bought this by over-connecting: it still calls the \emph{split} cliques two separate
components perfectly (split exact-match $1.00$, cross-connected rate $0.00$), so its
bridged-vs-split discrimination is $1.00$ at every clique size where the held-out model sat at chance
($0.50$). The cross block stays symmetric (directional-disagreement rate $\approx0$), as before ---
so the new ability is genuine \emph{propagation} of the one bridge, not a degree-driven ``dense means
connected'' shortcut. (The only residual blemish is a single $n=40$ seed whose whole-graph
exact-match at the largest clique is $0.71$ rather than $1.00$, from a few stray within-clique entries;
its cross block and discrimination are still $1.00$.) This is the cleaner of the two outcomes:
\textbf{the Section~\ref{sec:res-base} collapse was a data gap, not a hard capacity wall.}

\paragraph{What this does, and does not, settle.} It settles the open caveat of the whole report:
the node budget of Sections~\ref{sec:res-base} and~\ref{sec:res-sim} is a \emph{learned heuristic},
not a hard property of the $L=2$ trunk. The model defaults to a stuck, breadth-first ball on
dense-clique-plus-bridge graphs only because that structure never appears in the ordinary mixed
stream; the moment the data demand it, the same architecture propagates the bridge across a clique of
$20$ nodes without trouble. This is consistent with --- and reinforces --- the picture from
Section~\ref{sec:res-sim}, where merely \emph{changing the read-out} already shifted the budget
(knee $c\approx7\to11$--$12$ at $n=40$): the budget is malleable along two independent levers,
the output map and the training distribution, which is exactly what one expects of a \emph{soft}
data/architecture artefact and \emph{not} of a hard wall.

What it does \emph{not} do is move the distance-bounded capacity itself. Every target here sits at
distance $\le 3$, so this experiment only shows that the trunk can be \emph{taught} a
\emph{within-capacity} skill it had skipped; it says nothing about whether it could ever cross
\emph{beyond} $3^L=9$ hops, which the depth sweep of Section~\ref{sec:res-depth} (an $L\in\{1,3\}$
version of the same probe) is meant to test by asking whether the knee tracks $3^L$.
The honest summary is therefore the one the matrix-power hypothesis predicts: the architecture's real
ceiling is the distance-$3^L$ wall of Reports~III--IV; the bounded-traversal ``node budget'' that
looked like a competing, second limit is not structural at all --- it dissolves under a different
read-out and under the right data, leaving distance-bounded matrix powering as the mechanism the
$L=2$ model is actually built around.

The question we set out with (Section~\ref{sec:question}) was binary: does the $L=2$ transformer
solve connectivity by \emph{matrix powering} --- a parallel, distance-bounded aggregation that
reaches $3^L$ hops at once --- or by a \emph{bounded traversal} (a DFS or BFS that visits a fixed
number of nodes from a start and then stops)? The two predict opposite things on graphs that are
\emph{short but wide}, and the bridged cliques were built precisely to pull them apart. We can now
give an answer, with the residual uncertainty stated honestly.

\paragraph{Depth-first search is rejected, twice over.} On the constructed bridged cliques
(Section~\ref{sec:res-base}) the model's cross block \emph{falls} from $1$ to $0$ as the cliques
grow, while the bounded-DFS oracle --- which dives across the bridge and reaches deeper as its budget
grows --- \emph{rises}; and a single-start DFS would leave a directional fingerprint
($\hat R_{ij}\neq\hat R_{ji}$) that the model simply does not have (disagreement rate $\approx0$,
because a transformer emits all of $\hat R$ in parallel). On the one \emph{natural} graph that
reproduces the bridged-clique structure --- the held-out barbell --- the capstone with all three
oracles (Section~\ref{sec:res-capstone}) finds the model following bounded-BFS ($0.61$--$0.74$ at
$n=40$), not bounded-DFS, on the entries where the two traversals disagree. DFS is out on both the
constructed and the natural graph; we never need it again.

\paragraph{Not a literal matrix-power machine, but the deviation is not a second capacity.} Taken at
face value, Section~\ref{sec:res-base} also rules out \emph{naive} matrix powering: the model loses
the cross block at an absolute node budget of $\sim6$--$7$, even though the bridge sits at distance
$\le3$ at every clique size, where matrix powering would never fail. That looked, at first, like a
\emph{second} hard limit living next to the distance-$3^L$ wall --- a visit-bounded ``node budget.''
The last two experiments show it is not. Changing only the output map shifts the budget
(Section~\ref{sec:res-sim}: knee $c\approx7\to11$--$12$ at $n=40$ under the similarity read-out), and
adding the structure to training removes it entirely (Section~\ref{sec:res-capacity}: the same $L=2$
model then propagates the bridge across a clique of $20$, on the matrix-power oracle, every seed).
A limit that moves with the read-out and dissolves with the data is a \emph{learned heuristic}, not
an architectural capacity. The model defaults to a stuck breadth-first ball on dense-clique-plus-bridge
inputs only because nothing in the ordinary training stream ever rewarded carrying one edge across a
dense block --- a \emph{data prior}, not a structural bottleneck.

\paragraph{Best reading: distance-bounded matrix powering, with a removable data-prior shortcut.} The
evidence converges on a single picture. The architecture's genuine ceiling is the distance-$3^L=9$
wall of Reports~III--IV --- a parallel, fixed-depth neighbourhood aggregation, the matrix-power
family. The bounded-traversal signature that the bridged cliques exposed is real but \emph{soft}: it
is the shortcut the model falls back on off-distribution, and it disappears under either of two
independent levers. Two further results point the same way. Denser training makes the model converge
\emph{faster}, not slower (Section~\ref{sec:res-density}), the opposite of what any genuinely
visit-bounded traversal would predict (``more nodes to walk past is harder''). And across the twelve
natural families (Section~\ref{sec:res-capstone}) the model tracks matrix powering wherever the test
has power to tell; the bounded-traversal signature is visible \emph{only} on graphs purpose-built to
expose it (the constructed bridged cliques and the barbell), and nowhere it is not. We therefore read
the model as \emph{distance-bounded matrix powering} --- not a bounded DFS, not a bounded BFS --- with
the apparent ``node budget'' being a data-induced heuristic rather than a competing mechanism.

\paragraph{What we have not shown.} The honest caveats are two. First, all of our evidence is
\emph{behavioural}: the model's answers \emph{resemble} matrix powering, but we have not opened the
trunk to confirm it \emph{computes} powers of $A+I$ --- a mechanistic probe of the attention maps and
the per-layer embeddings (the geometry sketched in Reports~III--IV) is the obvious next step, and
would turn ``resembles'' into ``computes.'' Second, the capacity-vs-data-prior test
(Section~\ref{sec:res-capacity}) only taught a \emph{within-capacity} skill --- every bridge sat at
distance $\le3$ --- so it cannot, on its own, say whether training could push the model \emph{past}
$3^L$; the depth sweep of Section~\ref{sec:res-depth} ($L\in\{1,3\}$ on the same probe) is the
clean test of whether the knee tracks $3^L$. Neither caveat changes the verdict on the prof's
question. The transformer learns the \emph{algorithmic}, parallel, distance-bounded solution, not a
sequential bounded traversal --- and what once looked like a traversal-style node budget turns out to
be a removable artefact of the training data, not the way the model computes.
